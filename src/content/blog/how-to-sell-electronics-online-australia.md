This guide covers how to sell electronics online: Australia's compliance basics, product choice, listings, warranties, channels, sale seasons, reviews, returns and after-sales care. Australians research gadgets carefully. They compare battery life and compatibility, read reviews, watch setup videos and check that a product has the right plug and compliance marks before they pay. Familiar retailers such as JB Hi-Fi set expectations on range, price and help after the sale, so an online brand has to be just as clear and just as easy to deal with. Getting the rules right first also keeps listings live and away from takedowns.

## Step 1: Understand the Australian compliance basics

Treat this section as a general overview rather than legal advice. Rules depend on the exact product, and they change, so confirm your obligations with a testing lab or regulatory adviser before you import stock.

### Electrical safety and the RCM mark

Much consumer electrical equipment falls under the Electrical Equipment Safety System (EESS), run by the state and territory electrical safety regulators. In general terms, the responsible supplier, usually the Australian importer or manufacturer, registers on a national database. Equipment classed as higher risk must also be registered with evidence of compliance, while lower-risk items still need to be electrically safe. Compliant products carry the Regulatory Compliance Mark (RCM). Mains-powered products also need the Australian plug type, so check this with factories that mainly supply other markets.

### ACMA labelling

The Australian Communications and Media Authority (ACMA) sets rules for electromagnetic compatibility, radiocommunications and telecommunications equipment. Bluetooth earbuds, smart speakers, Wi-Fi devices and many other products are affected. The RCM mark is also used to show compliance with these rules, and suppliers generally keep records supporting the claim.

### Button and coin batteries

Products that contain or come with button or coin batteries, such as remotes, trackers, scales and some wearables, must meet mandatory safety and information standards. Broadly, that means secure battery compartments, child-resistant packaging for loose batteries, and clear warnings on the product, packaging, instructions and online listings. The ACCC treats this area seriously because of the risk to young children.

### E-waste and battery stewardship

Several states restrict electronic waste going to landfill. Televisions, computers and some accessories are covered by a national recycling scheme, and a voluntary battery stewardship scheme helps households recycle batteries. Your obligations depend on what and how much you sell, but telling buyers how to recycle old devices and batteries is sensible either way.

### Product safety and recalls

Suppliers must report serious injuries, illnesses or deaths associated with consumer goods to the ACCC, and recalls are published on Product Safety Australia. Keep batch and supplier records so you can trace any problem quickly.

### Compliance checklist

| Item | Ask before ordering stock |
|---|---|
| EESS classification | Is this product higher, medium or lower risk? |
| Responsible supplier | Who is registered, and is it us? |
| RCM and ACMA evidence | Do we hold test reports for this exact model? |
| Plug and voltage | Does it ship with the Australian plug and suit local supply? |
| Button batteries | Are compartments secure and warnings in place? |
| Recycling information | How will buyers dispose of the device and battery? |

## Step 2: Choose products and positioning

Pick categories where you can explain a real difference in a sentence, such as longer battery life for commuters, a charger that suits a particular set of devices, or a speaker tough enough for camping. Accessories and small gadgets are often easier first launches than high-risk electrical products. Read the three-star reviews on leading Amazon AU listings in your category; they show what buyers wish the current products did better.

### Research before you order stock

Before committing to a production run, check four things. First, how crowded the category is on Amazon AU and Google Shopping, and whether listings next to yours are from known brands or many lookalikes. Second, whether you can explain your difference in a short video. Third, how heavy and fragile the product is, because freight and breakage across a large country affect margin. Fourth, how much compliance work the model needs. A product that scores well on all four is a stronger first launch than one with exciting specs and a long list of unknowns.

## Step 3: Write spec-led listings in plain English

### Titles

Include the brand, product type, the key spec and the main compatibility: "Wireless earbuds, 30-hour battery case, Bluetooth 5.3, for iPhone and Android". Keep model numbers visible, because many buyers search for them.

### Bullet points

Lead with the two or three specs that matter most in your category and explain each in daily-use terms, such as hours of playback on a commute or how long a charge takes. Add compatibility, what is in the box and the warranty period.

### Images and video

Show ports, buttons and size in a hand, not only renders. A short setup or pairing clip answers questions a spec sheet cannot.

### Spec table

Publish a full table on your own store, and compare your models side by side so buyers choose the right one first time.

## Step 4: Make warranty and consumer rights clear

Under the Australian Consumer Law, buyers are entitled to a repair, replacement or refund when a product has a major failure, and those consumer rights apply regardless of any warranty you offer. Your warranty against defects is additional, and its documents need specific mandatory wording. State the period, what is covered and how to claim. Never imply that buyers have no remedy once your warranty ends, and do not sell extended warranties by misdescribing what the law already provides. Have an adviser review the wording.

### Retail as context

JB Hi-Fi and other large retailers shape what buyers expect from range, demos and after-sales help. You compete by being clearer and more helpful online, not by matching their range.

## Step 6: Plan for Australian sale seasons

- **Back to school, late January.** Laptop accessories, headphones and chargers for students.
- **Click Frenzy.** A major online sale moment for tech. Prepare deals and stock early.
- **EOFY in June.** Many buyers plan work-from-home purchases before the tax year ends.
- **Father's Day, September.** Gadgets are a common gift; promote delivery cut-off dates.
- **Black Friday, Christmas and Boxing Day.** The biggest window for electronics. Choose hero models, agree deals and build reviews beforehand.

Click costs rise in each window, so protect margin with bundles rather than deep cuts on new models.

## Step 7: Collect genuine reviews

Ask every buyer for a review through the channels each marketplace allows, after they have had time to use the product. Never offer incentives for positive reviews, never post fake ones and never suppress negative ones; the ACCC has acted against misleading review practices. Reply to critical reviews with practical help.

Reviews that mention a specific use, such as calls on a noisy train or battery life on a long flight, persuade more than generic praise. A post-purchase email that asks how the buyer uses the product, sent a couple of weeks after delivery, tends to draw out that detail. Read every review for patterns: repeated complaints about pairing or instructions point to a support fix, not a product fault.

## Step 8: Manage returns and fraud

Electronics attract some return abuse, such as swapped serial numbers or used items sent back as new. Record serial numbers before dispatch, photograph high-value items, and inspect returns before refunds on change-of-mind returns. Keep checks fair and quick, and never use them to refuse remedies buyers are entitled to.

### Protection checklist

- Serial numbers recorded per order
- Tamper-evident seals on packaging
- Clear change-of-mind policy separate from consumer rights wording
- Inspection steps documented for returned items

## Step 9: Support customers after the sale

Many items returned as faulty were simply not set up correctly. Publish setup guides, pairing videos, firmware update steps and troubleshooting FAQs, and link to them in the delivery email. Include recycling information for old devices and batteries. Good support content also ranks on Google.com.au and is often quoted by AI assistants.

Make it easy to reach a person, too. A support email or chat answered within one business day, a simple claims form and clear instructions for sending a faulty item back reduce frustration and protect your ratings. Track support questions by model each month; a spike usually means a listing, manual or firmware version needs attention.

## Common mistakes

- Ordering stock before confirming compliance for the exact model
- Listings without the plug type, compatibility or battery warnings
- Warranty wording that implies buyers have no other rights
- Deep discounts on new launches during big sale events
- No setup help, leading to avoidable returns and one-star reviews
