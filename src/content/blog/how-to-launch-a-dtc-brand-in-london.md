If you are working out how to launch a DTC brand in London, you are in a good city to try. London shoppers buy online constantly, discover brands through creators and markets, and expect fast delivery and easy returns. Competition is also fierce, and costs such as storage, staff and space are higher than elsewhere in the UK. This guide walks a new direct-to-consumer brand through the work in order: setting up the business, the store, delivery, launch marketing and the first months after launch.

We are an ecommerce growth agency, not accountants or solicitors. Treat the legal and tax points here as a general overview and confirm them with a qualified adviser.

## Step 1: set up the business properly

A few decisions made in the first fortnight save a lot of untangling later.

### Company formation

Many founders register a private limited company with Companies House. It keeps business and personal money apart, and suppliers, marketplaces and payment providers tend to treat a company as more established. Others start as sole traders and incorporate later. Each route has different tax and filing duties, so a short session with an accountant is worth it before you choose.

### VAT in general terms

You must register for VAT once your taxable turnover passes the registration threshold, and you can register voluntarily earlier. Voluntary registration can suit brands that buy a lot of stock up front, because they can usually reclaim VAT on purchases. Once registered, the prices you show to consumers should include VAT. Decide your position before you set launch prices, because adding VAT later squeezes your margin or forces a price rise.

### The rest of the paperwork

- A business bank account and a payment provider linked to your store
- Product liability and public liability insurance, especially if you plan pop-ups
- Trademark searches on your brand name before you print packaging
- Product rules for your category, such as cosmetics notification or toy safety marking
- A privacy policy and cookie consent that reflect UK GDPR

## Step 2: choose a range you can explain in one line

London is noisy. A brand that launches with a short range and a clear reason to exist is easier to remember than one with forty products. Pick three to five hero products that share one idea, check that each leaves a healthy margin after VAT, delivery, packaging, payment fees and returns, and hold the rest back. Our [profit margin calculator](/tools/profit-margin-calculator/) helps you test prices before you commit to stock.

## Step 3: build a store for UK shoppers

Most new DTC brands launch on Shopify because it handles VAT-inclusive pricing, UK payment options and fulfilment integrations without custom code. Whatever you choose, the store should:

- Show prices in pounds, including VAT, with delivery costs visible before checkout
- Offer the payment methods UK shoppers expect, including cards, digital wallets and pay-later options such as Klarna or Clearpay
- Use British spelling and sizes, and explain materials, ingredients or care plainly
- Carry clear delivery, returns and contact pages, linked from every product
- Load quickly on a phone on a busy train, which is where many London shoppers browse

Write product pages that answer real questions. Search engines and AI assistants pick up clear, specific pages, and they keep bringing visitors long after launch.

## Step 4: sort fulfilment and delivery around London

Delivery is part of your brand. London shoppers compare you with large retailers that deliver next day, so plan carefully.

### Packing yourself or using a 3PL

Packing orders yourself is sensible for the first weeks. You learn which packaging protects the product and which unboxing details customers mention. Once orders are steady, a third-party logistics partner (3PL) usually makes sense. Many sit around the M25, in east London, the Lea Valley, Park Royal and towards the Midlands. When you compare them, ask about:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Does it integrate with your store? | Orders should flow in without manual copying |
| What are the courier cut-off times? | A later cut-off means more next-day deliveries |
| How are returns handled? | Inspection and restocking speed affect cash flow |
| Can it cope with peaks? | Black Friday and Christmas can multiply volume |
| How are charges structured? | Storage, picking and packaging all add up |

### Couriers and same-day options

Most brands use a mix of Royal Mail for smaller parcels and tracked services, Evri for low-cost home delivery and DPD for timed, premium delivery with a delivery window. In London you can also offer same-day or evening delivery through courier apps or a 3PL with a London base. It costs more, so many brands offer it as a paid upgrade or a gifting option before Christmas, Valentine's Day and Mother's Day.

## Step 5: write clear returns terms under UK consumer law

UK online buyers generally have a cancellation period, usually 14 days from delivery, in which they can change their mind, with exceptions such as personalised items and opened hygiene-sealed goods. Separately, goods must be as described, of satisfactory quality and fit for purpose under the Consumer Rights Act. Put your policy in plain English, explain who pays for return postage, and make the process easy. A smooth return often brings a second order; a hard one brings a bad review.

## Step 6: launch with London creators, press and a pop-up

Launching locally gives you early buyers, reviews and content before you spend heavily on ads.

### London creators

Send product to a short list of London creators whose audiences match your buyer, and let them use it properly before posting. Micro-creators with engaged local followings often produce more useful content than large accounts. Agree usage rights so you can reuse their videos in ads, and make sure every paid or gifted post is labelled as an ad under ASA and CAP guidance.

### Press and local media

London has a dense layer of lifestyle titles, newsletters and neighbourhood publications. A short founder story, good product photography and a clear reason the brand exists give writers something to work with. Pitch gift guides early: editors plan Christmas round-ups months ahead.

### Pop-ups and markets

A weekend stall or short-term shop lets people touch the product and tells you which questions come up again and again. We cover this in detail in our guide to [running a pop-up shop in London](/blog/how-to-run-a-pop-up-shop-in-london/).

## Step 7: add paid ads once you have proof

Once you have early reviews, creator content and a store that converts, paid social becomes far more efficient. Start Meta ads with a few creatives built from real customer and creator footage, a modest daily budget and simple targeting across London and the wider UK. Add Google Shopping and search for people who already know what they want. ## How to launch a DTC brand in London: a checklist

- [ ] Company, bank account and VAT position agreed with an accountant
- [ ] Insurance and category product rules covered
- [ ] Three to five hero products with tested margins
- [ ] Store with VAT-inclusive pricing, UK payments and clear policies
- [ ] 3PL or packing plan, couriers and a same-day option decided
- [ ] Returns policy written to UK consumer rules
- [ ] Creator seeding list, press list and pop-up date
- [ ] Email sign-up on the store with a simple welcome series
- [ ] Ad account, tracking and analytics tested before launch day

## Common mistakes London founders make

- Launching too many products and spreading stock and attention thin
- Setting prices without allowing for VAT, delivery and returns
- Signing a long lease or big warehouse contract before demand is proven
- Gifting product to creators without agreeing ad labelling or usage rights
- Spending on broad ads before the store converts and reviews exist
- Ignoring the email list until after the launch spike has passed

## How long a launch usually takes

For a brand with product ready, a sensible launch often runs over two to three months: a few weeks for business setup and the store, a few weeks for fulfilment and creator seeding, then a launch window built around a market date or a seasonal moment. Launching just before Black Friday is tempting but crowded and expensive; a quieter month gives your first buyers more attention.

## The first 90 days after launch

The launch spike fades quickly, and what you do next decides whether the brand keeps growing. In the first three months, focus on a handful of habits:

1. **Read every review and support message.** Recurring questions point to missing product information or packaging problems.
2. **Watch repeat purchase timing.** If customers reorder after a few weeks, set up reminder emails around that point.
3. **Keep feeding creators.** Send new products or seasonal bundles to the creators whose content sold most.
4. **Test one ad idea at a time.** Change the hook, the offer or the audience, not all three at once.
5. **Review delivery data.** Late or damaged parcels show up in reviews before they show up in reports.

Keep a simple weekly sheet of orders, new customers, returning customers, ad spend and margin, so decisions rest on numbers rather than mood.
