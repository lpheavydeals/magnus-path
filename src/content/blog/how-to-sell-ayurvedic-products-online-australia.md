Anyone working out how to sell Ayurvedic products online Australia-wide soon learns that the herbs are the easy part. In an Indian shop, a bhringraj oil, a triphala churna and a tulsi tea sit on the same shelf. In Australia, those three products can fall under three different sets of rules: cosmetics, therapeutic goods and food. The category decides what you can say, where you can advertise and whether you can sell the product at all. This guide is for Australian founders, health food retailers moving online and family businesses that already sell Ayurvedic goods at markets or community grocers.

We describe the rules in general terms. We are an ecommerce growth team, not regulatory advisers, so confirm the detail for your own products with a TGA consultant, your sponsor or a lawyer before launch.

## Step 1: sort your range before you write a single page

Start with a spreadsheet of every product, its ingredients, its form and the words currently on its label. Then place each one in a likely bucket.

| Product type | Likely bucket in Australia | What it means online |
|---|---|---|
| Hair oils, body oils, soaps, ubtan face packs | Cosmetic | Full ingredient list; no therapeutic claims |
| Herbal teas, spice blends, ghee | Food | Food Standards Code applies, including limits on health claims |
| Tablets, capsules, churnas sold for health | Therapeutic good, often a listed complementary medicine | Needs ARTG listing and an Australian sponsor before supply |
| Chyawanprash, herbal jams and pastes | Depends on ingredients and claims | Get advice before listing |
| Mineral or metal-based formulas such as bhasmas | Higher risk | Many brands leave these out of an Australian range |

Wording can move a product from one bucket to another. A sesame massage oil described as nourishing for dry skin reads as a cosmetic. The same oil sold "for joint pain relief" starts to look like a therapeutic good, with all the obligations that brings. Decide the category first, then write to fit it.

## Step 2: understand listed complementary medicines

Most lower-risk herbal medicines in Australia are listed on the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods (ARTG). Broadly, a listed medicine:

- has an Australian sponsor who is legally responsible for it and holds the evidence behind its claims
- carries an AUST L number on the label
- may only use indications chosen from the TGA's permitted list, such as wording about traditional use in Ayurvedic medicine
- uses ingredients the TGA allows in listed medicines, and is made by a manufacturer that meets manufacturing requirements

Listing is not the same as the TGA checking that a product works. That is why it is unwise to write "TGA approved" anywhere. Show the AUST L number, quote the permitted indications accurately and keep your copy within them.

### Questions to ask your sponsor

- Which indications are on each product's ARTG entry, word for word?
- Which warning statements must appear on the label and in ads?
- Which products in our catalogue are not listed and must not be sold yet?
- Who reviews new ads and product pages before they go live?

## Step 3: cosmetic and food rules still matter

Products on the cosmetic side follow a different path. Australia has a mandatory standard for cosmetic ingredient labelling, so every oil, soap and face pack should list its ingredients in the expected order. If your business imports or manufactures cosmetics, you may also need to register with AICIS, the Australian Industrial Chemicals Introduction Scheme, which oversees chemical ingredients including those in cosmetics. Check whether this applies to you before your first shipment arrives.

Teas, spice blends and ghee sold as foods fall under the Food Standards Code, which limits health claims and requires country of origin labelling for most packaged foods. A tulsi tea can describe its taste, origin and ritual, but should not promise a health outcome unless that claim fits the food rules.

## Step 4: advertise within the rules

### The Therapeutic Goods Advertising Code

Ads for listed medicines, including product pages, emails and social posts, must follow the Therapeutic Goods Advertising Code. In general terms, claims must match the ARTG entry, references to serious diseases are restricted, required health warnings must be shown, and testimonials from people who are paid or gifted products are restricted. That last point rules out most standard influencer briefs for listed medicines.

### Consumer law and the AANA codes

For every category, the Australian Consumer Law prohibits misleading claims, and the ACCC watches health and "natural" claims closely. Ad Standards handles complaints under the AANA codes, which also expect paid content to be clearly disclosed. Meta, Google and Amazon AU add their own health policies on top.

### Practical wording swaps

| Avoid | Write instead |
|---|---|
| "Stops hair fall" | "A traditional herbal oil for a weekly scalp massage" |
| "Boosts immunity" | "A classical recipe made with amla, ghee and honey" |
| "Detox your body" | "Part of a seasonal routine described in Ayurvedic texts" |
| "Clinically proven" (without trials) | Ingredient source, batch testing and how to use it |

## How to sell Ayurvedic products online Australia shoppers trust

A store that explains itself sells better than one that promises a lot. Two groups visit: South Asian families who already know the products, and wellness shoppers who have heard of ashwagandha but not of doshas. Pages need to serve both.

### What every product page needs

- Ingredients with common and botanical names, and where the herbs are grown
- The category made clear: AUST L number, ingredient list or food label details
- How to use it, how often and for how long a pack usually lasts
- Warnings, and who should check with a health professional first
- Batch testing information, including heavy metals for herbal medicines
- Delivery times, returns and contact details that are easy to find

Prices should be shown including GST, and Afterpay or Zip can help with larger bundles.

### Content that brings search traffic

Australians search for plain questions: what triphala is, how to do abhyanga at home, whether ashwagandha tea tastes bitter. Write careful, useful answers, link them to the matching products and keep them free of disease claims. Over time, these guides bring buyers who are already curious. Our [Ayurveda marketing page for Australia](/australia/ayurveda-marketing-agency/) explains how we plan this content alongside ads.

### Photos, video and proof of quality

Show the product in use, not only the jar. A short clip of oil being warmed and massaged into the scalp, or a churna stirred into warm water, answers questions that a paragraph cannot. Film in ordinary Australian homes and bathrooms so newcomers can picture the ritual in their own week.

Quality is the other half of trust. Australian buyers have read news stories about imported herbal medicines with contaminants, so publish what you can: the farm or region the herbs come from, the manufacturer's name, and a summary of batch testing for heavy metals and microbes. Reviews that mention texture, smell and how a product fitted into a routine help far more than star ratings alone, so ask for them directly a few weeks after delivery.

Beauty retailers such as Mecca and department stores such as Myer and David Jones are not the starting point for most Ayurvedic brands, but they shape how Australians expect premium wellness packaging and product information to look. Pharmacies and health food stores set the comparison for herbal medicines.

## Step 6: delivery, returns and consumer rights

Oils leak, glass jars break and powders spill, so pack for long trips. Australia Post, Sendle and couriers all handle health and beauty parcels, but delivery to Perth, Darwin and regional towns takes longer than city-to-city, so state realistic times by region. Free shipping thresholds work well for bundles; set yours with care, since herbal jars are heavy.

Change-of-mind returns on opened ingestible or skin products are a policy choice, and many stores exclude them for hygiene reasons. That choice does not affect the consumer rights under the Australian Consumer Law, which apply when a product is faulty, not as described or damaged in transit. Write your returns page so both are clear.

## Step 7: build repeat orders and plan the year

Ayurvedic products are used daily, which makes repeat purchase the real business. A churna or hair oil runs out on a predictable cycle, so reminders timed to that cycle feel helpful rather than pushy. Subscriptions, refill packs and routine bundles raise the value of each customer. Plan stock and creative around the Australian calendar:

- **Diwali:** the main gifting moment for Indian-Australian families; hampers and gift boxes
- **Click Frenzy, Black Friday and Christmas:** gift sets for wellness shoppers; publish delivery cut-offs early
- **Boxing Day and EOFY in June:** clear older batches before expiry dates get close
- **Valentine's Day:** massage oils and couples' self-care sets
- **Mother's Day in May:** gift boxes of oils, face packs and teas
- **Wedding season:** ubtan and pre-wedding care kits for South Asian weddings
