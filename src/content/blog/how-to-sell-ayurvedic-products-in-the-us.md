This guide explains how to sell Ayurvedic products in the USA when the products are made in India, or anywhere outside the US. It is for Indian manufacturers opening a US market, US founders who source formulations from Indian factories, and diaspora retailers who want to move beyond a few suitcases of stock. We cover routes into the country, FDA classification, registration, prior notice, testing, labels, positioning and where to sell once stock lands.

We are an ecommerce growth team, not customs brokers, lawyers or regulatory consultants. The points below describe the general picture. Rules and tariffs change, so confirm every step with a licensed customs broker and a US regulatory adviser.

## Choose your route into the US

How goods enter the country shapes every later decision, from who holds legal responsibility to how fast you can restock.

| Route | How it works | Suits | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| US importer or distributor | A US company buys from you and handles import, compliance and wholesale | Makers who want US reach without a US company | Less control over price, brand and customer data |
| Your own US company plus a warehouse | You import in bulk as importer of record and sell from US stock | Brands building a long-term US business | You carry the compliance duties directly |
| Amazon-first | Stock is imported and sent into Amazon's network | Brands with strong diaspora demand | Amazon's supplement documents and storage rules |
| Parcels from India | Each order ships directly to the buyer | Testing demand with a small range | Slow delivery, changed low-value parcel rules, awkward returns |

Many brands begin with a distributor or small direct shipments to test demand, then set up their own US company once orders are steady. Whatever you choose, someone has to act as the importer of record, the party legally responsible for the goods at the border.

## Classify every product for the FDA

The FDA has no Ayurvedic category. It decides how to regulate a product from its intended use, which comes mainly from your labels, inserts, website and ads.

- **Dietary supplements** such as capsules, tablets and many powders. They need a Supplement Facts panel, must be made under the supplement manufacturing rules, and may carry structure/function statements with the FDA disclaimer. A new dietary ingredient can need premarket notification.
- **Cosmetics** such as hair oils, face oils, soaps and ubtans. Claims stay with appearance and cleansing. Facility registration and product listing duties now apply to many cosmetic businesses.
- **Foods** such as herbal teas, ghee and spice blends, with nutrition labeling and food facility rules.
- **Unapproved drugs.** Any product presented as treating, curing or preventing a disease falls here, whatever its tradition. That includes inserts and leaflets packed inside the box.

## Importing: the general picture

### Paperwork for each shipment

Your broker will ask for a commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading or air waybill, and a product description detailed enough to choose the right tariff classification. For herbal products, keep the certificate of analysis, ingredient list and label artwork for each lot in the same folder. Descriptions that differ between the invoice, the label and the FDA filing are a common cause of questions at the border, so use one master product sheet and copy from it every time. If a shipment is held, the speed of your reply depends on how quickly you can send these files.

### Registration and a US agent

Foreign facilities that make, process, pack or hold food for US consumers, including dietary supplements, generally need FDA food facility registration and must name a US agent. Cosmetic facilities have their own registration and listing duties. Registration is not approval: it simply tells the FDA who you are.

### Prior notice

Food shipments, including supplements, generally need prior notice filed with the FDA before they arrive. Your customs broker usually files it alongside the customs entry, but you should know which of your shipments need it and make sure product descriptions match the paperwork exactly.

### Importer duties

US importers of food and supplements usually need a Foreign Supplier Verification Program, which documents how they check that their foreign suppliers meet US safety standards. If you are your own importer, this sits with you; if a distributor imports, they will ask you for the records that support it.

### Detention, refusal and import alerts

The FDA can examine, detain or refuse shipments that appear not to comply. Some Ayurvedic and herbal products have been placed on import alerts in the past, mainly over heavy metals and unapproved drug claims, which allows detention without physical examination. A detained container costs time and storage, so prevention matters more than appeals.

### Duties and parcel rules

Tariff rates on goods from India, and the treatment of low-value parcels sent straight to consumers, have changed more than once recently. Ask your broker for the current position before you set prices, and review it again before each peak season.

## Test every batch before it ships

Heavy metals are the issue US regulators, journalists and careful shoppers raise first. The FDA has warned about lead, mercury and arsenic in some Ayurvedic products, so testing is the foundation of a US business, not an extra.

- Test each batch at an independent lab for lead, mercury, arsenic and cadmium, plus microbes.
- For botanicals, add pesticide residues and, for some ingredients, aflatoxins.
- Keep a certificate of analysis for every lot and match it to the lot code on the pack.
- Consider re-testing a sample at a US lab for your first shipments, so you can compare results.
- Ask whether California's Proposition 65 warning rules apply to any product sold to California buyers.

Publish results on your website by lot code. That page will do more for trust than any line of ad copy.

## Make labels ready for US shelves

Indian labels rarely work in the US without changes. A US-ready label generally needs:

- English throughout, with US-style formatting for the product's category
- A Supplement Facts panel, cosmetic ingredient declaration or Nutrition Facts panel as appropriate
- Net quantity in US customary units and metric units
- The name and place of business of the manufacturer, packer or distributor
- Country of origin marking, such as "Made in India"
- Allergen declarations where relevant, for example milk in ghee or tree nuts in some blends
- The FDA disclaimer next to any structure/function statement
- No disease claims on the pack, the box or the leaflet inside it

Print a lot code and expiry or use-by date on every unit. Amazon and many retailers will also ask for clear label images that match the physical product.

## Position the brand for American buyers

Two audiences buy Ayurvedic products in the US. Diaspora shoppers know the herbs and often the brands, and they want genuine formulas at fair prices with fresh stock. Mainstream wellness shoppers may know one or two ingredients and need a simple reason to choose you over a familiar supplement brand.

For both groups, "made in India" can be a strength when it is backed by evidence: where the herbs grow, how they are processed, how batches are tested. Avoid suggesting that imported means more authentic or more effective. ## Where to sell once stock lands

- **Your own store.** The place to explain sourcing, testing and rituals, and to run subscriptions.
- **Amazon US.** Strong diaspora demand, but supplement sellers must supply documents such as test results and label images. Our guide on [selling on Amazon USA from India](/blog/how-to-sell-on-amazon-usa-from-india/) covers account setup.
- **Walmart Marketplace.** A second marketplace once operations are steady, with its own approval process.
- **TikTok Shop.** Creator-led discovery, with close review of health claims.
- **Indian grocery stores and online grocers.** A wholesale channel that reaches diaspora households directly.

Start with your own store and one marketplace. Adding channels before reviews, reorders and stock planning are under control usually spreads a small team too thin, and a stock-out on Amazon can cost search ranking that takes weeks to rebuild.

## Work out the landed cost

Before setting retail prices, add up every cost between your factory and the customer's door:

- Product cost and US-format packaging
- Testing for every lot, in India and possibly in the US
- Freight, insurance, customs brokerage and duties
- FDA-related services, such as a US agent
- Warehousing, pick and pack, and marketplace fees
- Returns, expired stock and damaged goods
- Advertising to reach buyers who have not heard of you

## Mistakes to avoid

- Shipping Indian-market packs with disease claims printed on the box
- Sending a wide range before one product has proved itself
- Relying on the factory's test results alone, with no independent check
- Treating registration as a stamp of approval in marketing copy
- Forgetting leaflets, QR-linked pages and old social posts when cleaning up claims
- Running out of stock before Diwali or the holidays because sea freight took longer than planned

## How to sell Ayurvedic products in the USA: a step-by-step plan

1. Pick your route: distributor, your own US company, Amazon-first or direct parcels.
2. Classify each product for the FDA and drop anything that only works with disease claims.
3. Confirm facility registration, a US agent and any cosmetic listing duties.
4. Test each batch at an independent lab and organize results by lot code.
5. Redesign labels and inserts for US rules.
6. Line up a customs broker for entries and prior notice.
7. Ship a small first range and sell it through your store and one marketplace.
8. Plan the US calendar: January wellness, Prime Day in July, Diwali in October or November, then Black Friday, Cyber Monday and the holidays.
9. Review sales, reorders and reviews after the first season before widening the range.
