Diwali marketing in the UK is no longer a niche activity. Supermarkets, fashion retailers and beauty brands now run Diwali and Eid campaigns, and shoppers can tell quickly which ones were made with care and which were a quick colour change. For an online brand, the opportunity is real, but so is the risk of getting dates, tone or delivery wrong. This guide covers how to plan Diwali marketing, UK Ramadan and Eid campaigns, respectful creative, the advertising rules, budget phasing and the delivery cut-offs that decide the result.

Rules mentioned here are general guidance only. Check them with an adviser for your own campaigns.

## Understand the festivals and their dates

Many South Asian and Muslim festivals follow lunar calendars, so their dates move each year in the Gregorian calendar.

- **Diwali** is celebrated by Hindus, Sikhs and Jains, usually in October or November. In 2026, most calendars put Diwali on 8 November. Sikhs mark Bandi Chhor Divas at the same time. Some regional calendars differ by a day, so confirm before you print a date on creative.
- **Navratri** comes before Diwali, with nine nights of garba and dandiya in many Gujarati communities, and Durga Puja for many Bengali families in the same period.
- **Ramadan** is the month of fasting. Shopping tends to move to evenings and late nights, and builds through the month towards Eid. Ramadan is expected to begin around 8 February 2027.
- **Eid al-Fitr** marks the end of Ramadan and is expected around 9 to 10 March 2027. **Eid al-Adha** follows about two months later, expected around 16 May 2027. Both depend on moon sighting and can move by a day or two, and families or mosques may celebrate on different days.
- **Vaisakhi**, usually in mid-April, is a major Sikh and Punjabi festival and a useful context for clothing and food brands, though it is a smaller gifting moment.

## Decide what your campaign is for

Before choosing colours and hashtags, decide what the campaign should achieve:

| Goal | Typical products | What to measure |
|---|---|---|
| Gifting sales | Hampers, fragrance, jewellery, sweets, home decor | Orders before cut-off, gift-wrap take-up |
| Outfit sales | Festive wear, kidswear, menswear, accessories | Orders by size, returns after the day |
| New customers | Hero products, sets, sampling | New buyer share, cost per new customer |
| Loyalty | Early access, family bundles | Repeat orders from past festive buyers |

A campaign that tries to do all four usually does none of them well. Pick one or two goals per festival.

## Build respectful, specific creative

Respect is mostly about specificity. A generic "festive season" banner with lanterns, diyas and a crescent moon mixed together tells buyers you did not ask anyone.

### Do

- Ask people who celebrate the festival to review your ideas and copy early, and pay them for that time.
- Show real family moments: preparing food, choosing outfits, visiting relatives, giving gifts.
- Use the right greeting for the right festival, such as "Happy Diwali", "Ramadan Mubarak" or "Eid Mubarak".
- Cast people of different ages, regions and body types, including mixed families.
- Recognise that not everyone celebrates in the same way or on the same day.

### Avoid

- Mixing symbols from different religions in one visual.
- Using sacred texts, deities or prayer imagery as decoration for a discount.
- Jokes about fasting, worship or modest dress.
- Food or drink advertising that makes light of fasting during Ramadan.
- Suggesting that someone must spend a lot to celebrate properly.
- Copy that assumes the viewer's religion, such as "As a Muslim, you'll love this".

## Follow the advertising rules

UK ads are covered by the CAP Code, enforced by the Advertising Standards Authority (ASA). For festive campaigns, the main points are:

- **Harm and offence**: ads must not cause serious or widespread offence, including on grounds of religion or belief.
- **Pricing claims**: "was" prices and percentage discounts need a genuine basis, and the offer terms should be clear.
- **Delivery claims**: "arrives before Eid" must be true for the postcodes and dates you advertise.
- **Promotions**: prize draws and giveaways need clear terms, closing dates and fair administration.
- **Influencer posts**: paid or gifted creator content must be clearly labelled as advertising.

Ad platforms add their own rules. Meta does not allow targeting by sensitive categories such as religion, and its policies forbid ad text that asserts or implies a person's religion or ethnicity. Plan targeting around interests and behaviour, not identity.

## Phase your ad budget

Festive auctions get busier as the day approaches, and Diwali falls close to Black Friday, so costs can rise quickly. The figure at the top of this guide shows a five-stage plan.

### A practical schedule

1. **Six to eight weeks before**: lock stock, offers, landing pages and delivery cut-offs.
2. **Three to four weeks before**: test several hooks and products on small budgets. Collect video viewers and site visitors for remarketing.
3. **Final two weeks**: move most spend to the winning ads and products. For Ramadan campaigns, expect evening and late-night activity, and give the last ten days extra weight before Eid.
4. **Cut-off week**: run "order by" reminders, push express delivery and gift cards.
5. **After the day**: thank customers, show gift cards for late buyers and invite reviews.

### Channels to consider

- **Meta and Instagram** for outfit and gifting discovery, with Reels and carousels of edits.
- **TikTok** for try-ons, unboxing and "what I'm wearing for Eid" content from creators.
- **Google Search and Shopping** for intent searches such as "Diwali gift hamper delivery London".
- **Email and WhatsApp** for past buyers, who are often the cheapest festive sales you will make.

## Work with creators and community partners

Creators who celebrate the festival themselves bring credibility that brand-made content cannot. Choose people whose audience matches your buyers, brief them on the product and the rules, and let them show how they really prepare: outfit choices for Eid morning, a Diwali gift haul, cooking for iftar with your homeware. Label paid and gifted posts clearly.

Community partners matter too. Local charities, temples, gurdwaras, mosques and community groups often run events and fundraisers around these festivals. If you support them, do it genuinely and without turning the relationship into an advert. Many brands also give a share of festive sales to a cause; if you do, say exactly how it works and keep the promise.

## Make gifting easy

Gifting drives much of the Diwali and Eid basket, so remove friction:

- Gift edits by recipient (parents, hosts, children, colleagues) and by budget band.
- Gift wrap, a handwritten-style note and the option to hide prices on the packing slip.
- Delivery to a different address, with the date shown clearly.
- Corporate gifting for staff and clients, with a simple bulk order form.
- Digital gift cards for anyone who misses the cut-off.

## Get delivery cut-offs right

The festival day is not your deadline. Your last reliable delivery date is.

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Fixed date (Diwali) | Publish an "order by" date per delivery option, working back from the day |
| Moon-based date (Eid) | Base cut-offs on the earliest likely date and say so |
| Made-to-order outfits | Close orders earlier, with lead time shown on the page |
| Imports from abroad | Allow for customs delays and order stock well ahead |
| London buyers | Offer same-day or next-day delivery as a late option where you can |

Show the cut-off on product pages, the basket and in emails. After it passes, switch ads to gift cards and in-stock items with express delivery, or pause them. ## Measure and learn for next year

Write down what happened while it is fresh:

- Which products and edits sold, and which ran out.
- When orders peaked compared with the festival day.
- Which creative and hooks worked, by channel.
- How many orders missed the cut-off or arrived late.
- Return rates on outfits after the day.

These notes become next year's plan, and they matter more than any general benchmark.

- [ ] Festival dates confirmed, with moon-based dates marked as expected

- [ ] Creative reviewed by people who celebrate it

- [ ] Budget split across prepare, test, scale and cut-off stages
