This is a practical guide to how to start a cosmetics brand: USA rules, suppliers, money and launch, in the order you will need them. The US beauty market is large and crowded, and new makeup brands now launch on TikTok as often as in stores. That makes starting easier than it was, and standing out harder. The steps below cover niche, manufacturing, compliance, packaging, brand, unit economics, channels, launch and operations, so you spend your first budget where it counts.

## Step 1: Choose a niche and a first range

### Pick a clear buyer

"Makeup for everyone" is a slogan, not a plan. Choose one buyer you can describe in detail. Examples: a complexion range for deep skin tones with clear undertone guidance, sweat-resistant makeup for people who work out, quick five-minute products for busy professionals, or fragrance-free formulas for sensitive eyes.

### Keep the first range small

Every shade multiplies stock, photography, swatches and the risk of dead inventory. A single hero product in a well-chosen shade range, or a short routine around one look, is easier to fund and explain. You can expand when you know what sells and reorders.

### Check the market

Spend time on TikTok, Amazon, Sephora and Ulta product pages. Read the one- and two-star reviews of products close to your idea; they tell you what buyers wish existed. Note shade gaps, packaging complaints and finishes people ask for.

## Step 2: Find a contract manufacturer

Most new US cosmetics brands do not own a factory. They work with a contract manufacturer, either developing a custom formula or adapting a private label base with their own shades.

When comparing manufacturers, ask about:

- Experience with your product type and finish
- Good manufacturing practices and MoCRA facility registration
- Stability, compatibility and preservative efficacy testing
- Which color additives they use and whether they are approved for your intended use
- Minimum order quantities per shade
- Lead times for first runs and reorders
- Who handles product listing and adverse event records
- Whether they can produce OTC products, if you later add SPF

Order samples, wear-test them yourself and with a small group, and check how shades look on different skin tones in daylight before you commit.

## Step 3: Sort out the business, labels and legal basics

This section is general information, not legal advice. A regulatory adviser and accountant should confirm what applies to you.

### Business setup

Most founders form an LLC or corporation, get an EIN, open a business bank account and buy product liability insurance, which many retailers and marketplaces ask for. File a trademark application with the USPTO early; you need a registered or pending trademark to join Amazon Brand Registry. Buy UPC barcodes from GS1 US so your products can be listed on major marketplaces.

### FDA, MoCRA and color additives

Cosmetics do not need FDA approval before sale, but MoCRA added duties: facility registration, product listing, safety substantiation, serious adverse event reporting and a US contact on the label for those reports, with exemptions for some small businesses. Color additives are the exception to "no approval". They must be approved for their intended use, some need FDA batch testing, and some colors allowed in lipstick are not allowed near the eyes. If you sell one product for lips, cheeks and eyes, every color in it must be allowed for all three.

### Labels

A cosmetic label generally needs an identity statement, net contents in US and metric units, the ingredient list in descending order of predominance, the name and place of business of the manufacturer or distributor, and any required warnings. Small packages have some flexibility. If a product claims SPF, it becomes an OTC drug with a Drug Facts panel and different rules, so many brands leave tinted SPF for a later phase.

### California Prop 65

California requires warnings for products that expose buyers to listed chemicals above set levels. Some pigments and ingredients can raise questions, so ask your manufacturer for a Prop 65 review before printing packaging.

### Claims

The FTC expects claims to be truthful and supported before they appear. "Long-wear", "waterproof", "non-comedogenic", "vegan" and "cruelty-free" all need evidence on file. "Made in USA" claims follow FTC rules that generally require all or virtually all of the product to be made in the US.

## Step 4: Design packaging that sells and ships

Makeup packaging has to look good on camera and survive parcel networks.

- **Shade visibility:** a window, colored cap or clear shade name helps buyers and warehouse staff alike.
- **Protection:** compacts crack and lipsticks snap. Use inserts and drop-test packed orders.
- **Hygiene seals:** tamper seals reassure buyers and reduce opened-and-returned stock.
- **Hazmat:** nail polish, aerosols and some alcohol-based products count as dangerous goods for shipping and on Amazon, with extra paperwork and limits.
- **Sustainability claims:** "recyclable" claims are judged under FTC Green Guides, so check local recycling reality before printing them.

## Step 5: Build a brand people remember

A brand is more than a logo. Decide on:

- A name you can trademark and a matching domain and social handles
- A clear promise, written in one sentence, that your products can keep
- A visual style that shows shades accurately, not just beautifully
- A voice for captions, emails and replies to comments
- Inclusive imagery that reflects every skin tone in your range

Founder content works well in US beauty: why you started, what you tested, which shades took ten attempts. Be honest; buyers are quick to spot invented stories.

## Step 6: Work out unit economics

Before ordering stock, calculate what each sale earns on each channel. Include:

| Cost | Notes |
|---|---|
| Cost of goods | Formula, filling, component, by shade if prices differ |
| Packaging | Primary pack, carton, inserts, seals |
| Fulfillment | Pick-and-pack, storage, shipping materials |
| Shipping | Carrier rates by zone; free-shipping thresholds |
| Payment fees | Card and wallet processing |
| Marketplace fees | Amazon referral and FBA fees, TikTok Shop commission |
| Returns and damage | Opened makeup usually cannot be resold |
| Marketing | Ad cost per order, creator fees and affiliate commission |

Also budget for sales tax compliance: after the Wayfair decision, states can require remote sellers to collect tax once sales pass their economic nexus thresholds, while marketplaces generally collect tax on their own orders.

## Step 7: Build your store and listings

### Your own store

Shopify is a common choice for US beauty brands. Make sure it has:

- Swatches on several skin tones, shot in consistent daylight
- Undertone guidance and a shade-match quiz
- Reviews that show the reviewer's usual shade
- Clear shipping times, return rules and hygiene policy
- Fast checkout with Shop Pay, Apple Pay, Google Pay and PayPal

### Marketplaces

TikTok Shop suits visual, shade-led launches. Amazon US suits products shoppers already search for. Walmart Marketplace can come later, and Target Plus is invitation-only. Sephora and Ulta are retail goals for some brands, but study their pages now as a benchmark for shade information and imagery.

## Step 8: Plan the launch

1. **Seed creators** a few weeks before launch, matching skin tones to your shade range.
2. **Build a waitlist** with email and, with consent, SMS sign-ups.
3. **Launch day:** creator posts, a live session on TikTok Shop and paid ads behind the strongest content.
4. **Second wave:** new creators, new looks and restock news a few weeks later.

Every gifted or paid creator post needs clear disclosure under the FTC Endorsement Guides. Time your launch away from peaks you cannot staff: Black Friday, Cyber Monday and the holidays are crowded and expensive, while Valentine's Day suits lip launches and Mother's Day in May suits gift sets.

## Step 9: Run operations and control returns

- Forecast stock by shade, not just by product; popular shades sell out first.
- Track returns by shade. A shade returned far more often than its neighbors usually has a misleading swatch.
- Keep batch records and a simple process for logging and reporting adverse events.
- Reply to reviews and comments quickly, and add common answers to product pages.

## A realistic first-year timeline

Founders often underestimate how long the groundwork takes. Formula development or adaptation, stability testing, packaging design and the first production run can take several months, and custom packaging components often have the longest lead times of all. Plan backward from your launch date:

1. **Months one to three:** niche, manufacturer search, samples and wear tests.
2. **Months three to six:** formula approval, testing, packaging design, trademark filing and business setup.
3. **Months six to nine:** production, labels, photography, store build, marketplace applications and creator seeding.
4. **After launch:** restock planning by shade, second-wave creators and a decision on the next product.

These are typical ranges, not promises; your manufacturer's lead times decide the real schedule. Keep some cash in reserve for a faster-than-expected reorder, since running out of a shade that creators are talking about wastes the attention you paid for.

## Mistakes to avoid in your first year

- Launching too many shades before demand is clear
- Using colors not approved for the eye area in multi-use products
- Making long-wear or clean claims without evidence
- Running out of the most popular shades right after launch
- Ignoring sales tax until a state notice arrives
