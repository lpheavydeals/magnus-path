## Sort every product into a US category first

US law has no category called Ayurveda. The FDA looks at two things: how a product is used and what you say it does. That means a single herb can end up in several places.

| Product example | Usual US category | What it triggers |
|---|---|---|
| Ashwagandha capsules, triphala powder, herbal tablets | Dietary supplement | Supplement Facts panel, structure/function claims with the disclaimer, supplement manufacturing rules |
| Bhringraj hair oil, kumkumadi face oil, ubtan | Cosmetic | Ingredient declaration, appearance-only claims, cosmetic registration and listing duties |
| Tulsi tea, ghee, spice blends | Conventional food | Nutrition labeling and food facility rules |
| Any of these sold "for arthritis" or "to treat diabetes" | Unapproved drug | Disease claims take the product outside the supplement and cosmetic routes |

Work through your catalog one product at a time before you write a single listing. The category decides the label layout, the words you can use in ads and the documents a marketplace will ask for. Some products sit on a borderline, such as a herbal balm marketed for sore muscles or a jam-like preparation that could be a food or a supplement. Settle those with your adviser before launch, not after a listing is pulled.

Cosmetics now carry more paperwork than they used to. The Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act (MoCRA) added facility registration, product listing, adverse event reporting and safety substantiation duties for many cosmetic makers and brand owners. Ask which of these apply to your oils and skincare.

## Claims: what DSHEA allows

The Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA) is the law behind most herbal capsules and powders on US shelves. It allows a supplement to describe how an ingredient affects the normal structure or function of the body, but not to promise to treat illness.

### Structure/function statements

"Supports restful sleep", "helps maintain healthy digestion" and "supports a normal response to everyday stress" are typical structure/function statements. Each one needs the standard FDA disclaimer on the label: the statement has not been evaluated by the FDA, and the product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease. The FDA also expects to be notified of these statements shortly after the product goes on sale, which your adviser can handle.

### Disease claims hide in more places than copy

Naming a disease or its typical symptoms in a way that suggests treatment is a disease claim. That includes product names, images, hashtags, blog posts linked from the product page and customer reviews you choose to feature. A five-star review saying a powder "healed my eczema", pinned to the top of the page, is now part of your marketing.

### Evidence for advertising

The FTC regulates advertising and expects health-related claims to be backed by competent and reliable scientific evidence that matches what the claim actually says. A sentence about tradition should be framed as tradition, without implying a proven outcome.

| Risky wording | Safer direction |
|---|---|
| Treats insomnia | Part of a calm evening routine; supports restful sleep* |
| Detoxes your liver | A traditional digestive blend, taken after meals |
| Clears acne | Leaves skin looking clearer and feeling soft (cosmetic) |
| Stops hair fall | Leaves hair looking fuller and glossier (cosmetic) |

*With the FDA disclaimer on the label. Even the safer versions need evidence and adviser review.

## Purity, heavy metals and batch testing

Anyone researching Ayurveda in the US quickly meets FDA consumer warnings about lead, mercury and arsenic found in some Ayurvedic products, particularly certain imports. Some classical rasa shastra preparations include metals or minerals by design, and many US sellers simply leave those out of their range. Your marketing cannot pretend the concern does not exist. It can show how your own products are handled.

- **Test every batch.** Use an independent lab for heavy metals and microbes, and for pesticide residues on botanicals where relevant.
- **Link results to the pack.** Print a lot code and let buyers look up the matching certificate of analysis on your site.
- **Explain your process.** A short "how we test" page should say what is tested, how often and by whom, in words a non-scientist follows.
- **Check state rules.** California's Proposition 65 can require warnings for some substances; ask whether it applies.
- **Know your manufacturer.** Supplements must be made under the FDA's current good manufacturing practice rules for dietary supplements. Ask your contract manufacturer how they comply and keep the records.
- **Drop vague purity words.** "Pure", "clean" and "toxin-free" invite questions you may not be able to answer. Say what you tested instead.

## Build a store that answers a cautious buyer

Your own Shopify store is where a hesitant shopper decides whether to trust you. Marketplaces show a product; your store explains a practice.

### What each product page needs

- One sentence that tells a newcomer what the product is
- How to take or use it, how much, when and for how long a pack lasts
- Full ingredients, with Sanskrit names alongside common English names
- The Supplement Facts or cosmetic ingredient panel as an image and as readable text
- A link to batch test results
- A note to speak with a doctor first if pregnant, nursing or taking medication
- A subscription option set to the real usage cycle

### Two audiences, one set of facts

Indian-American and wider South Asian shoppers often grew up with these products. They look for familiar formulas, fresh stock and the feeling of home. Mainstream wellness shoppers may know ashwagandha from social video and nothing else, so they need a short explanation and a reason to trust a brand they have never heard of. Write separate landing pages and ads for each group, but keep claims and facts identical.

### Content that earns search and AI visits

People ask Google and AI assistants questions such as "what is triphala", "how to take ashwagandha" or "what is ghee used for in Ayurveda" every day. Careful explainers that separate traditional use from what research supports can bring visits for years. ## Choose your US channels in order

| Channel | Where it helps | What to prepare |
|---|---|---|
| Your Shopify store | Education, subscriptions, customer data | Herb explainers, testing page, reorder flows |
| Amazon US | Diaspora searches, Prime shoppers | Supplement documents, label images, test results |
| Walmart Marketplace | A second marketplace with broad reach | Seller approval and category requirements |
| TikTok Shop | Discovery through creators | Strict health-claim review, careful creator briefs |
| Etsy | Handmade soaps, balms and herbal skincare | Cosmetic claims only; Etsy restricts medical claims |

On Amazon US, supplement sellers are asked for documents such as third-party test results and label images that match the listing. Missing files, restricted ingredients or disease wording in a title can get a listing suppressed. Read the current category rules in Seller Central before you send stock, because they change. Walmart Marketplace has its own approval steps, so treat it as a second marketplace rather than a launch channel.

## Fulfillment, shelf life and returns

Most brands selling to US buyers at volume hold stock inside the country, either with a third-party logistics provider or in Amazon's network. A few points matter for herbal products:

- Track lot numbers and expiry dates through fulfillment, and ship oldest stock first.
- Use leak-resistant caps and sealed inner bags for oils and ghee.
- Keep powders dry; humidity in summer transit can clump them.
- Write a returns policy that suits consumables: most brands do not resell opened products but replace damaged or wrong items.

## Grow through repeat buying

Ayurvedic products are usually daily habits, so the business is built on the second, third and tenth order.

### Acquisition

Search ads on herb names and routine terms, Meta and TikTok ads that teach rather than promise, and creators who already talk about wellness routines. Creators must disclose paid, gifted or affiliate relationships clearly under the FTC's endorsement rules, and they should not make claims you could not make yourself. Ad platforms also restrict health claims and some supplement ingredients, so expect reviews and plan alternatives.

### Retention

- Subscriptions with an easy skip or pause
- Reorder reminders timed to pack size, such as day 24 on a 30-day jar
- Morning and evening routine bundles
- Short how-to emails in the first two weeks after purchase

### The US calendar

January wellness resolutions, spring routines, Prime Day in July, back-to-school routines in the fall, cold-season interest from October, Black Friday and Cyber Monday, and holiday gifting. Diwali, in October or November, matters for diaspora buyers ordering gift boxes and family packs.

### Measuring

Track reorder rate, subscription retention after three and six months, refund reasons and review themes. Compare what a customer is worth over a year with what it cost to acquire them; our [LTV to CAC calculator](/tools/ltv-cac-calculator/) helps you check whether a channel is worth scaling.

## How to sell Ayurvedic products online (USA): a launch checklist

- Every product mapped to supplement, cosmetic or food, with borderline items cleared by an adviser
- Labels reviewed: panels, disclaimer, lot codes, warnings, net quantity in US units
- Structure/function statements filed where needed, and disease language removed everywhere
- Batch testing in place and results linked from the site
- Product pages with usage, full ingredients and a testing link
- Amazon supplement documents prepared before listing
- Subscriptions and reorder reminders set up before ads start
- Creator briefs that include FTC disclosure and claim limits
- Stock and campaigns planned for January, Prime Day, Diwali and the holidays
