When you sit down to write a skincare brand marketing strategy, Australia gives you an unusual starting point. Most buyers grew up with sun safety campaigns, check SPF numbers closely and can find a decent serum at the supermarket. That makes them easy to talk to about ingredients and hard to impress with vague promises. This guide sets out a practical plan for an independent label: where to stand, who to talk to, which channels to use, what the rules expect and how to measure whether it is working.

It is general guidance, not legal advice. Check product-specific questions with your regulatory adviser.

## Start with a position Australians can repeat

A position is the sentence a customer would use to describe you to a friend. If they cannot say it, your ads have to work much harder.

### Positions that tend to suit this market

- **Climate-first.** Products designed for humidity, dry winters or long days in the sun, with routines that change by season.
- **A single skin concern.** Sensitive, reactive, oily or mature skin, explained better than the big brands bother to.
- **One active, done well.** A stated strength of one ingredient, with honest guidance on how to introduce it.
- **SPF built into the routine.** Daily wear products that people actually want to put on, sold with clear regulatory status.
- **Ingredient or sourcing values.** Australian botanicals, refills or recyclable packs, but only where you can back every claim.

### Check the position against what you can prove

Write down each claim your position relies on, then list the evidence beside it. Under the Australian Consumer Law, claims must not mislead, whether they appear in an ad, on a page or in a creator's caption. Green and ethical claims get particular attention from the ACCC. If the evidence is thin, change the wording before you build a campaign around it.

## Know which buyers you are serving

Different buyers arrive at different moments and need different proof.

| Buyer | What sets them off | What they need to see |
|---|---|---|
| Label reader | Saw an active trending, wants to compare | Strength, full ingredient list, pairing advice |
| Sensitive-skin buyer | Reacted to another product | Patch-test advice, fragrance notes, honest reviews |
| Sun-safety regular | Summer, travel, a new outdoor routine | Regulatory status, SPF as tested, texture under makeup |
| Gift buyer | Christmas, Mother's Day, a birthday | Sets, wrapping, delivery cut-off dates, easy exchanges |
| Returning customer | Bottle running low | Quick reorder, refill size, loyalty points |

Most young brands try to talk to all five at once. Pick the two that suit your position and build pages and ads for them first.

## Build an education layer before buying traffic

Paid traffic is expensive when it lands on a page that cannot answer questions. Fix the content first.

### Product pages

Each hero product page should explain the key ingredients and their role, the skin types it suits, how to use it, what to avoid pairing it with and what someone might notice over a few weeks. Add a short texture video and reviews that mention skin type.

### Guides and search

Routine guides, ingredient explainers and climate-specific advice bring in shoppers searching Google.com.au and asking AI assistants for recommendations. Write for the questions buyers ask, such as how to fit a retinoid into a routine in summer or which sunscreen textures sit well under makeup in humid weather. These pages also give ads a stronger place to land.

### Short video

Reels and TikTok clips that show application, finish and a real morning routine do more than polished studio shots. Keep filters off the skin.

## Match channels to what the buyer already knows

Split your spend between finding new people and catching those who are already looking.

### Meta and TikTok for discovery

Use routine videos, texture close-ups and creator content to introduce the brand. Avoid hooks that point at personal attributes, such as asking viewers about their breakouts. Send traffic to a page built for that audience, not your home page.

### Google Search and Shopping for intent

Buyers typing a product type plus a concern into Google are close to ordering. A clean Shopping feed with accurate titles and GST-inclusive AUD prices often does more than clever ad copy.

### Amazon AU, TikTok Shop and other marketplaces

Amazon AU suits hero products with reviews, where shoppers compare quickly and want fast delivery. TikTok Shop, where available to your business, lets a creator video act as the shop window. eBay AU and Kogan can move gift sets and end-of-line stock. Retailers such as Mecca, Sephora, Priceline and Chemist Warehouse set price expectations that buyers carry with them, so keep your own pricing believable against them.

## Creators and reviews within the rules

Creators can carry a skincare launch, but the rules on disclosure are clear.

- **Disclose clearly.** The AANA code expects advertising to be distinguishable as advertising, which covers paid, gifted and affiliate posts. Ad Standards hears complaints.
- **Keep claims honest.** A creator's words count as your advertising. Brief them on what the product does and does not do.
- **Watch sunscreen testimonials.** If a product is a therapeutic good, the TGA advertising code restricts testimonials, including from paid creators.
- **Handle reviews properly.** Never write, buy or selectively hide reviews. The ACCC treats fake and filtered reviews as misleading.

Get written agreements that cover disclosure, content reuse in ads and how long you can use each piece.

## Use paid sampling to lower the risk of trying

A buyer with reactive skin is reluctant to spend on a full bottle. A small paid mini or discovery kit lowers that risk without filling your list with people who only want freebies.

The sample is only half the plan. Follow it with usage tips in the first few days, a check-in before the mini runs out and a credit towards the full size. Measure mini-to-full-size conversion by product and by the channel that sold the mini, then put budget behind the kits that convert.

## Plan for the second order from day one

Repeat orders decide whether a skincare brand makes money. Build them into the plan.

- **Refill reminders** timed to how long a bottle actually lasts with daily use.
- **Routine bundles** that pair a fast-moving product with a slower one.
- **Subscriptions** with an easy pause or skip option, so they do not feel like a trap.
- **Loyalty points** that reward the second order more than the tenth.
- **Afterpay** on bundles and sets, where the larger basket justifies the fee.

## Keep offers from wearing down the brand

Australian shoppers see a lot of beauty discounting. Pharmacy chains run frequent beauty promotions, large retailers offer gift-with-purchase deals and sale events seem to arrive every few weeks. A small label cannot win a price war against that, and trying usually teaches buyers to wait for the next code.

Use offers that reward the behaviour you want instead:

- **Value through sets.** A routine bundle or a duo of a fast-moving product feels generous without cutting the price of a single bottle.
- **Gifts that start a habit.** A mini of a complementary product, added to a first order, gives a reason to try the next step in the routine.
- **Loyalty over codes.** Points or early access for returning customers protect margin better than site-wide discounts.
- **Short, planned sale windows.** Pick a few dates, such as Click Frenzy, Black Friday and EOFY, and keep prices steady between them.

Check each promotion against your margin before it goes live, including payment and marketplace fees. A discount that looks small on the price tag can remove most of the profit from an order once postage and ad cost are counted.

### Where retail fits later

Some labels will eventually want space in a specialist beauty retailer or a pharmacy chain. Retail buyers tend to look for steady direct sales, strong reviews and a clear position. A disciplined online strategy builds all three, so treat retail as a possible later chapter rather than the starting point.

## Your skincare brand marketing strategy: Australia 90-day plan

Turn the ideas above into a calendar. A realistic first quarter for a small label might look like this:

1. **Weeks 1 to 2:** settle the position, map claims against evidence and confirm which products are cosmetics and which are therapeutic goods.
2. **Weeks 3 to 5:** rebuild the hero product pages, write three or four routine guides and film texture videos.
3. **Weeks 6 to 8:** test Meta, TikTok and Google Shopping with small budgets, one audience at a time.
4. **Weeks 9 to 10:** launch paid minis with follow-up emails, and list hero products on Amazon AU.
5. **Weeks 11 to 13:** review repeat rate and cost per order by product, then move budget to what people buy again.

Line the year up with Australian dates: sunscreen and lightweight textures from spring, Click Frenzy and Black Friday in November, Christmas and Boxing Day, Valentine's Day, Mother's Day in May and EOFY sales in June for clearing older stock. Richer textures can lead in the dry winter months.

## Measure what matters

| Measure | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Cost per first order, by product | Shows which products are cheap to introduce |
| Mini-to-full-size rate | Tells you whether sampling pays |
| Repeat rate at 60 and 90 days | The real test of product fit |
| Refund and complaint reasons | Early warning on claims, texture or delivery |
| Profit per customer over time | Decides how much you can spend to acquire |

Review these monthly. Return on ad spend alone flatters products that sell once and never again.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Running ads before product pages can answer a careful buyer's questions.
- Writing sunscreen copy the same way as moisturiser copy.
- Paying creators for reach without written disclosure terms.
- Giving away free samples to anyone who asks.
- Cutting prices on marketplaces below your own store.
- Judging a campaign on first orders while ignoring reorders.
