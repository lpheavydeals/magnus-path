This Amazon PPC guide is written for Indian sellers who are new to advertising on Amazon and want a clear system rather than guesswork. Amazon PPC (pay-per-click) lets you show your products at the top of search results and on competitor product pages, and you pay only when a shopper clicks. Done well, it brings the first sales, reviews and organic rank. Done badly, it burns money quietly. Below we cover campaign types, structure, match types, negatives, bids, the key metrics and a weekly routine you can follow.

## Amazon ad campaign types

Amazon offers three main self-serve ad types for sellers:

| Campaign type | Where ads appear | Who can use it | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sponsored Products | Search results and product pages | All professional sellers | Driving sales of individual products |
| Sponsored Brands | Top of search with logo, headline and several products or video | Brand-registered sellers | Brand awareness and cross-selling a range |
| Sponsored Display | Product pages, Amazon and partner placements, retargeting | Brand-registered sellers (check eligibility) | Retargeting and defending product pages |

For beginners, Sponsored Products is where most of the budget should go. It is closest to the purchase, easiest to manage and works without Brand Registry. Add Sponsored Brands and Display once your core campaigns are stable.

## Before you spend: get the listing right

Ads send traffic; the listing converts it. Before launching Amazon PPC for beginners in India, check that each product has:

- A clear title with the main search terms.
- Sharp images, including lifestyle and infographic images.
- Bullet points that answer buyer questions.
- Some reviews and a competitive price.
- Stock you can sustain for the campaign period.

Advertising a weak listing raises your cost per sale. If listings need work, our [Amazon SEO service](/amazon-seo/) covers listing optimisation.

## Auto vs manual campaigns

### Automatic campaigns

In an auto campaign, Amazon decides which searches and products to show your ad against, based on your listing. You can bid separately on four targeting groups: close match, loose match, substitutes and complements. Auto campaigns are your research engine. They find search terms you would not have guessed.

### Manual campaigns

In a manual campaign, you choose the keywords or products to target and set bids yourself. Manual campaigns give control: you can bid more on terms that convert and cut terms that do not.

### A simple Sponsored Products campaign structure

For each product or closely related product group, run:

1. **Auto campaign** for discovery.
2. **Manual broad or phrase campaign** for a set of researched keywords.
3. **Manual exact campaign** for proven, converting search terms.
4. **Product targeting campaign** for competitor ASINs and relevant categories.

Keep campaigns small and focused. One product or one tight product group per ad group makes data easy to read and bids easy to manage.

## Match types explained

Manual keyword campaigns use three match types:

- **Broad:** your ad can show for searches containing your keyword words in any order, plus related terms. Good for discovery, but wasteful if unmanaged.
- **Phrase:** the search must contain your keyword phrase in the same order, with words before or after. A balance of reach and control.
- **Exact:** the search must closely match your keyword, allowing small variations such as plurals. Most control, usually the best conversion.

A common approach is to discover with auto and broad, refine with phrase, and scale with exact.

## Search-term harvesting

Search-term harvesting is the habit that separates efficient accounts from wasteful ones. Every week:

1. Download the search term report from your auto, broad and phrase campaigns.
2. Find search terms with orders at an acceptable ACoS.
3. Add those terms as exact match keywords in your exact campaign.
4. Add the same terms as negative exact in the source campaign, so the two campaigns do not compete.

Over time, your spend moves from discovery towards proven terms with better control.

## Negative keywords

Negative keywords stop your ads showing for searches that waste money. Add negatives when a search term:

- Has clicks but no orders after a reasonable number of clicks.
- Is irrelevant, such as a different product, size or material you do not sell.
- Is a competitor brand term that does not convert for you.

Use negative exact for specific poor terms and negative phrase for words that are always irrelevant, such as "free" or a material you never stock. Review negatives carefully so you do not block good traffic.

## Bids and budgets

### Bidding strategies

Amazon offers dynamic bids that can lower only, raise and lower, or fixed bids. Beginners usually start with "down only" to limit risk. You can also add placement adjustments to bid more for top-of-search or product page placements if they convert better.

### Setting bids

Start near Amazon's suggested bid range, then adjust based on performance. Raise bids on keywords with a low ACoS and good conversion. Lower bids on keywords with a high ACoS. Change bids in small steps and give them time to show results.

### Budgets

Set daily budgets you can sustain for several weeks. If a profitable campaign runs out of budget early in the day, raise its budget. If a campaign spends fully with poor results, fix bids and negatives before adding money.

## ACoS, ROAS and TACoS: formulas and a worked example

These three metrics tell you whether your ads make sense.

- **ACoS (Advertising Cost of Sales)** = ad spend ÷ ad sales × 100
- **ROAS (Return on Ad Spend)** = ad sales ÷ ad spend
- **TACoS (Total Advertising Cost of Sales)** = ad spend ÷ total sales (ad + organic) × 100

ACoS vs ROAS is simply two views of the same ratio: ACoS of 25% equals ROAS of 4.

**Worked example (hypothetical numbers for illustration only):** a seller spends ₹10,000 on ads in a month. Those ads generate ₹40,000 in sales. Total sales for the product, including organic, are ₹1,00,000.

- ACoS = 10,000 ÷ 40,000 × 100 = **25%**
- ROAS = 40,000 ÷ 10,000 = **4**
- TACoS = 10,000 ÷ 1,00,000 × 100 = **10%**

You can check your own numbers with our [ROAS calculator](/tools/roas-calculator/).

### Break-even ACoS from your margin

Your break-even ACoS is your profit margin before advertising. Continuing the hypothetical example: a product sells for ₹1,000. After product cost, Amazon fees, shipping and taxes, the seller keeps ₹300 before ads. The margin before ads is 30%, so the break-even ACoS is **30%**.

- ACoS below 30%: the ads are profitable on those sales.
- ACoS at 30%: the ads break even.
- ACoS above 30%: each ad sale loses money, though it may still be worth it during a launch to build reviews and rank.

Set your target ACoS below break-even to leave profit. Work out your own fees from the current rates in Seller Central, because they vary by category and change over time.

### Why TACoS matters

ACoS judges campaigns. TACoS judges the business. If your TACoS falls over months while total sales grow, ads are helping you earn organic rank. If TACoS keeps rising, you depend on paid traffic, and profit will suffer.

## A weekly Amazon PPC optimisation routine

Consistency beats clever tricks. Follow this routine every week:

1. **Check spend vs budget** for each campaign. Note any that ran out early.
2. **Harvest search terms** from auto, broad and phrase campaigns into exact.
3. **Add negatives** for terms with clicks and no orders, or irrelevant terms.
4. **Adjust bids** on keywords well above or below your target ACoS.
5. **Review placements** and adjust top-of-search or product page modifiers.
6. **Check listings and stock**. Pause ads on out-of-stock or suppressed products.
7. **Track TACoS** and total sales, not just ACoS.
8. **Write down changes** so you can link results to actions.
