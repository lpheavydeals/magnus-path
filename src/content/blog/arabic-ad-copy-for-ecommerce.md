Arabic ad copy is where many UAE ecommerce campaigns quietly lose money. A brand writes a strong English headline, runs it through a translation tool, drops the result into the same left-to-right design and wonders why Arabic-speaking shoppers scroll past. Arabic readers notice at once when an ad was written for someone else. This guide covers the decisions that make Arabic ads work for online stores in Abu Dhabi, Dubai and across the Gulf: which kind of Arabic to use, how to transcreate rather than translate, how to design right-to-left creative, and how to target and test by language on Meta and Google.

## Why Arabic ad copy deserves its own process

The UAE is a bilingual market. Many Emirati families and Arab expats browse, search and buy in Arabic, while a large share of other residents shop in English. A store that sells to both cannot treat Arabic as an afterthought, for three reasons:

- **Trust.** Awkward Arabic signals a brand that does not really know its customers, which matters most when you ask a stranger to pay.
- **Clarity.** A literal translation can change what an offer means, such as whether delivery is free on every order or only above a basket value.
- **Cost.** Ads that feel foreign earn less attention, so you pay more for each purchase.

## Modern Standard Arabic or Gulf dialect?

Arabic has a shared written standard and many spoken dialects. For UAE ecommerce, the choice is usually between two.

| | Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) | Gulf dialect (Khaleeji) |
|---|---|---|
| How it feels | Clear, correct, a little formal | Warm, personal, conversational |
| Where it fits | Search ads, product pages, banners, longer text | Creator videos, Snapchat, TikTok, Instagram captions |
| Who understands it | Readers across the Arab world | Strongest with Gulf audiences |
| Risk | Can sound stiff if overdone | Can sound forced if the writer is not from the Gulf |

A practical rule: use simple, modern MSA for written ads and anything a shopper reads closely, and Gulf dialect for spoken video and casual captions. Never mix the two in a single line, and keep spelling of dialect words consistent across your account.

Arab expats from the Levant or Egypt may connect with their own dialects too. That is worth testing only once your core Arabic ads are working.

## Transcreation, not translation

Translation moves words from one language to another. Transcreation moves the idea, so the Arabic ad has the same effect on its reader that the English one has on its reader.

### Brief the writer on the idea

Give your fluent Arabic writer more than the English text:

1. The buyer you are speaking to and what they care about
2. The single message the ad must get across
3. The offer details that must not change, such as dates, conditions and delivery promises
4. Your brand's tone in both languages
5. Words to avoid, such as competitor names or claims you cannot support

### Adapt what does not carry over

- **Wordplay and idioms.** English puns rarely survive. Ask for a new line with the same feeling.
- **Gender.** Arabic verbs and adjectives change with the gender of the person addressed. Decide whether the ad speaks to women, men or everyone, and use the plural or a neutral construction when in doubt.
- **Calls to action.** "Shop now" can become several different Arabic phrases. Choose one per campaign and keep it consistent between ad and landing page.
- **Numbers and prices.** Decide whether to show Western digits or Arabic-Indic digits and stick to it. Many UAE retail ads use Western digits for prices with "درهم" or AED.
- **Punctuation.** Arabic uses its own comma (،) and question mark (؟). Mixed English and Arabic punctuation looks careless.

### Length

Arabic text can run shorter or longer than English depending on the phrase, and some Arabic fonts need more line height. Write for the space available instead of forcing a translation into an English layout.

## Right-to-left layouts and creative

Arabic reads right to left, and the whole design should follow.

### Static ads and carousels

- Mirror the layout so the headline starts on the right and the eye moves leftward to the product and call to action.
- Flip arrows, progress indicators and "swipe" cues. A carousel arrow pointing right tells an Arabic reader to go backwards.
- Leave logos and product packaging unmirrored; only the layout flips.
- Use a properly designed Arabic typeface. Some design tools break Arabic letter joining, so check the export, not just the editor.
- Avoid English text baked into images used for Arabic ads.

### Video

- Write Arabic subtitles rather than relying on auto-captions, and place them where they do not cover the product.
- Plan Arabic voice-over or a presenter speaking Arabic, rather than dubbing over English lip movement.
- Respect local norms on dress, modesty and family scenes. Real UAE homes and everyday settings usually work better than generic stock footage.

### The landing page

An Arabic ad that opens an English product page wastes the click. Send Arabic ads to the Arabic version of the page, with right-to-left layout, Arabic product names, sizes and delivery messages. ## Tone and respect

Arabic advertising in the UAE is generally warmer and more courteous than blunt English direct response. A few principles help:

- Be polite and specific rather than pushy. Overly aggressive urgency can feel disrespectful.
- Use religious phrases only where they are natural and appropriate, such as a seasonal greeting, and never as a joke or a sales gimmick.
- Show people, clothing and relationships in ways that fit local values.
- Keep claims accurate. UAE advertising standards from the UAE Media Council expect content to be truthful and in good taste, and influencers you pay need an advertiser permit.

## Language targeting on Meta and Google

### Meta

In the ad set audience, you can add Arabic as a language, which limits delivery to people who use Facebook or Instagram in Arabic. Because many bilingual users keep their apps in English, test two set-ups:

1. Arabic creative with Arabic language targeting
2. Arabic creative with broad targeting in the same locations

Meta can also show different language versions of one ad based on each person's language, which keeps the account tidy. Whichever method you use, report Arabic and English results separately.

### Google

Google Ads language targeting uses signals such as the user's language settings and the language of the search. Build separate Arabic search campaigns with Arabic keywords, Arabic ad text and Arabic landing pages. Check the search terms report often: shoppers may type product names in English inside Arabic queries, or use Arabic words written in Latin letters. Shopping and Performance Max campaigns need an Arabic product feed to show Arabic titles. Our [Google Ads team for UAE brands](/uae/google-ads-agency-dubai/) builds these as separate campaigns.

## Testing Arabic vs English ad copy

Treat language as a test, not an assumption.

1. **Keep everything else equal.** Same product, offer, budget, placements and time period.
2. **Tag your links.** Use our [UTM builder](/tools/utm-builder/) to label Arabic and English traffic so store analytics shows which language each order came from.
3. **Measure purchases, not clicks.** Compare cost per purchase, average order value and COD refusal rate by language.
4. **Give it enough time.** A few days rarely settles it; let each version reach a meaningful number of purchases.
5. **Retest by season.** Ramadan, Eid and National Day can shift which language performs.

## Working with a fluent Arabic writer

Your writer should be a fluent Arabic writer with experience in Gulf advertising, not just general translation. Ask for samples of ads they have written, agree a glossary of product and brand terms, and have a second fluent reader check anything high-stakes, such as a seasonal campaign or a new offer. Review the copy inside the finished creative, since many errors appear only after layout.

## Common Arabic ad copy mistakes

Most problems with Arabic ads are easy to spot once you know where to look:

- **Broken letters.** Arabic letters join differently depending on their position in a word. Design tools without proper Arabic support show them disconnected or reversed, which looks careless at once.
- **English word order.** A sentence that follows English structure reads as translated, even when every word is correct.
- **Mixed registers.** A formal MSA headline followed by a dialect call to action feels like two different brands.
- **Inconsistent product names.** If a product is called one thing in the ad, another in the catalogue and a third on the product page, shoppers lose confidence.
- **Offers that change meaning.** "Up to" discounts, minimum basket values and delivery conditions are easy to blur in translation. Check them line by line.
- **Forgotten small print.** Disclaimers, sizes and delivery times in the landing page footer need Arabic versions too.

### Channel-specific tips

Snapchat and TikTok reward short, spoken Arabic that sounds like a real person, so a Gulf creator speaking naturally usually beats a polished script. Instagram captions can carry slightly longer Arabic text, but keep the first line strong, since the rest is hidden. Search ads have tight character limits, so ask your writer for several short headline options rather than one long line.
