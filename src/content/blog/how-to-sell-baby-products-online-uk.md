Anyone planning how to sell baby products online UK parents will trust needs to accept one thing early: safety comes before marketing. New parents read labels twice, check parenting forums and compare against brands they already know from the high street. Toys, nursery furniture, toiletries and feeding products each sit under different rules, and some products, such as infant formula, face strict limits on promotion. This guide walks through safety, rules, product pages, channels, data and repeat orders in the order you will need them.

A note on scope: we help brands sell online and do not give legal or product safety advice. The rules sections are a broad map, so confirm your own products' position with a test house, trading standards or a qualified adviser.

## Step 1: Get product safety right first

### Standards and marking

Different baby products sit under different rules. In broad terms:

- **Toys** placed on the market in Great Britain are covered by the toy safety regulations. Expect to need conformity paperwork, a UKCA mark (or a CE mark where that is still recognised) and the name and address of the business responsible.
- **Nursery products** such as cots, cot mattresses, high chairs, baby walkers, car seats and pushchairs are tested against specific standards. Car seats also have their own approval system.
- **Baby toiletries**, including wash, cream and wipes with cosmetic claims, fall under UK cosmetics rules, which require a responsible person, a safety assessment and notification before sale.
- **Clothing and textiles** must meet general product safety and labelling rules, with extra care around cords and drawstrings on children's clothing and nightwear flammability.
- **Everything else** falls under general product safety law, which expects products to be safe in normal and foreseeable use.

If you import, you may take on legal duties as the importer, so keep technical files, test reports and declarations of conformity from suppliers.

### Materials and design

Parents look closely at materials. Be precise: food-grade silicone, organic cotton with the certification body named if you hold it, water-based paints. Avoid vague phrases such as "chemical-free" or "100% safe", which are both meaningless and likely to attract complaints.

### Age grading and warnings

Show age suitability clearly, with choking and small-parts warnings for under-threes where the rules call for them, supervision advice and assembly instructions. Make these visible on the product page, not only on the packaging.

## Step 2: Understand the rules on infant feeding and claims

### Infant formula and follow-on formula

Advertising infant formula is generally prohibited in the UK, and follow-on formula promotion is tightly restricted. Price promotions, free samples and certain kinds of contact with parents are also limited. If formula is part of your range, take specialist legal advice before listing or promoting it, and keep it out of ads, influencer content and promotional emails.

### Weaning foods and toddler snacks

Baby foods have their own composition and labelling rules. Rules restricting online advertising of less healthy food and drink have also been introduced in the UK, so check whether any snacks in your range are affected.

### Claims on toiletries and wellness products

Do not say a cream treats eczema or that a product prevents nappy rash unless it is properly authorised as a medicine for that purpose. Describe ingredients, texture, fragrance and how to use the product, and send parents to the NHS website when the question is really a medical one. The CAP code covers these claims on your own website as well as in ads.

## Step 3: Build product pages that answer parents' questions

A UK parent should find every safety and practical answer without contacting you.

| Section | What to include |
|---|---|
| Materials or ingredients | Full list, with sources or certifications you can evidence |
| Age and stage | Suitable ages, weight limits and developmental stage |
| Safety | Marks, standards met, warnings and supervision advice |
| Size and fit | Measurements, size charts by age, and fit for prams or cots |
| Use and care | Cleaning, sterilising, washing temperatures, storage |
| Delivery and returns | Dispatch times, courier, and which items cannot be returned once opened |

### Photos and content parents trust

Show products in real use: the sleepsuit on a baby, the high chair at a kitchen table, the toy in small hands. Include close-ups of seams, fastenings and labels. Short videos on folding a pushchair or fitting a cot sheet answer questions and reduce returns. Real parent reviews with photos are often more persuasive than polished shoots. Ask for them a few weeks after delivery, once the product has been used, and reply to concerns openly so other parents can see how you respond.

## Step 4: Choose your UK sales channels

### Your own store

Your own Shopify or WooCommerce store holds subscriptions, bundles, gift options and your relationship with each family. It is where detailed safety information and buying guides live. Show prices including VAT, offer the payment options UK parents expect, and make delivery times clear on every product page. Practical guides, such as choosing a cot mattress or what to pack for the maternity ward, bring parents back between purchases and give search engines and AI assistants something useful to quote. A gift-finder page organised by age and budget helps relatives who are unsure what to buy.

### Amazon UK

Many parents buy nappies, wipes and everyday essentials on Amazon UK, often through Subscribe & Save. Toys and some baby categories can need extra documents or approval, so plan for it. Watch for unauthorised sellers and check reviews closely, because a single safety complaint can affect a listing.

### Etsy and Not On The High Street

These suit personalised gifts, handmade toys, keepsakes and nursery prints. Personalised items are often excluded from the usual cancellation right, which you must explain before purchase. Handmade toys still need to meet toy safety rules.

### TikTok Shop UK, eBay and OnBuy

TikTok Shop suits clever feeding products, play items and creator-friendly gadgets. eBay and OnBuy add reach for established ranges. Check category rules for baby and toy products before listing.

Retailers such as John Lewis set parents' expectations for range and returns. Your pages should answer the same questions just as clearly.

## Step 5: Market responsibly to parents

- Advertise to parents and gift buyers, never to children.
- Avoid copy that implies you know someone's circumstances, such as "Just had a baby?".
- Use broad audiences with clearly relevant creative, as many sensitive targeting options have been removed.
- Never use fear to sell. Practical reassurance works better and keeps you within the CAP code.
- Ask creators to label paid or gifted content clearly and to follow the same claims rules as you.

## Step 6: Handle parents' data with care

Baby brands often collect due dates, children's birthdays and sizes. Under UK GDPR these are personal data, so:

- Collect only what you need for a stated purpose, such as sending the right size reminder.
- Get clear consent for marketing emails and texts, and offer an easy opt-out in every message.
- Let parents update or delete a child's details easily.
- Store data securely and limit who in your team can see it.
- If any part of your site is likely to be used by children, consider the ICO's children's code.

## Step 7: Plan repeat orders, life stages and gifting

### Repeat essentials

A family gets through nappies, wipes and wash in a fairly predictable number of weeks. Build a subscription around that rhythm, let parents swap sizes or pause from their account, and send a nudge shortly before a pack should run out.

### Life stages

Children change size and stage quickly. If a parent has shared their child's birth date and agreed to marketing, you can time messages to the moves they are about to make: up a nappy size, into bigger sleepsuits, onto weaning spoons, towards the next set of toys.

### Gifting

Relatives and friends buying for a shower, a naming day, a first birthday or Christmas want a set that is ready to give, a note they can personalise and confidence that it will arrive on time. Back to school adds a later moment for uniform basics, lunch kits and name labels.

## Step 8: Plan operations around the UK calendar

- **Black Friday to Christmas.** The biggest window for toys and gifts. Publish last order dates early.
- **Boxing Day and January.** Sales on bigger nursery items as families plan ahead.
- **Spring.** Mother's Day gifting and travel kit for summer holidays.
- **Late summer.** Back to school for older siblings and toddlers starting nursery.

Keep enough stock of essentials year-round, because a parent who finds you out of stock rarely waits.

## Common mistakes when selling baby products online

- **Listing before testing.** A product pulled for safety reasons damages trust far more than a delayed launch.
- **Borrowing claims from overseas brands.** Wording that is common in other markets may break UK rules on cosmetics, foods or advertising.
- **Relying on narrow pregnancy audiences.** Those targeting options have largely gone, and copy that hints at them can get ads rejected.
- **Hiding returns rules for hygiene items.** If opened products cannot be returned, say so clearly before checkout, not in an email afterwards.
- **Treating gift buyers like parents.** Grandparents and friends need different pages: gift sets, wrapping and delivery dates rather than refill reminders.
- **Running out of essentials.** Parents switch brands quickly when nappies or wipes are unavailable, and many never come back.
- **Forgetting the child grows.** Without size and stage planning, your happiest customers stop buying within months.
