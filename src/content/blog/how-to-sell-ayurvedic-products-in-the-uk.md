Plenty of brands want to know how to sell Ayurvedic products in the UK, and most of them start in the wrong place: they copy their Indian website, ship a few cartons and wait. Britain is a rewarding market for Ayurveda, with a large South Asian population and a wider public curious about herbs and rituals, but it has its own product categories, labelling rules and advertising standards. This guide is for Indian makers entering Britain, UK importers bringing Ayurvedic ranges in and British brands sourcing formulas from India.

We are an ecommerce growth team, not regulatory lawyers or customs brokers. Use this as a general overview and confirm each step with a qualified adviser before you commit stock or spend.

## Who buys Ayurveda in Britain

Two groups make up most of the demand. South Asian households, many raised on chyawanprash, triphala, turmeric milk and weekly hair oiling, already know what they want. They look for familiar names, authentic sourcing and easy reordering, and they buy heavily around Diwali, Eid and wedding season. The second group is broader: wellness-minded people who have found ashwagandha, abhyanga or tongue scraping through yoga studios, podcasts or social media. They need the idea explained before they buy.

Most entrants do well by serving the first group first. Demand is already there, word of mouth is strong and the products need less explaining. The second group grows the brand over time.

## How to sell Ayurvedic products in the UK: place every product on a UK route

India treats many Ayurvedic products as medicines under the AYUSH system. Britain has no equivalent category. Each product has to fit an existing UK route, and that depends on its ingredients, its form and, crucially, how it is presented.

### The cosmetics route

Hair oils, face packs, soaps, ubtan, body oils and lip balms are normally covered by the UK Cosmetics Regulation. Broadly, you need a UK Responsible Person, a cosmetic product safety report prepared by a qualified safety assessor, notification of the product before it goes on sale, and a label that meets UK requirements, including the ingredient list in the standard international naming format and the Responsible Person's UK address. Claims must stay cosmetic: cleansing, conditioning, fragrance, appearance.

### The food supplement route

Capsules, powders and tablets presented as food can be sold as food supplements. The importer is usually treated as a food business and registers with its local authority. Check every herb against what is already accepted as food in Britain, because some botanicals have no history of food use here and may count as novel foods needing authorisation. Health claims must come from the authorised list, and many popular herbs have no authorised claim, so packs and pages tend to describe ingredients and use instead.

### The traditional herbal registration route

Herbal medicines for minor conditions can be registered with the MHRA under the traditional herbal registration scheme, usually called THR. Applicants need evidence of long traditional use, data on quality and a manufacturer meeting good manufacturing practice. Once registered, a product shows the THR mark and can use only the narrow wording its registration permits. It is a slow, document-heavy route, usually kept for a few hero products with proven demand.

### Products to hold back

Formulas that only make sense as medicines but have no UK authorisation should not be sold here, however popular they are in India. Rasa shastra preparations containing metals or minerals are the hardest of all, because UK regulators have raised safety concerns about them before. Leave them out of your first range.

## Test every batch and keep the paperwork

Testing is your protection and your evidence. For each batch, commission an accredited laboratory to test for heavy metals, pesticide residues, microbial contamination and, where relevant, aflatoxins. Keep a certificate of analysis on file and make sure batch numbers on packs match the documents.

Amazon UK, retailers and importers may ask for these results, and so may enforcement officers if a complaint comes in. A brand that can produce clean results in a day looks far more credible than one that has to ask the factory.

## Rewrite labels and packaging for Britain

An Indian pack rarely works unchanged. Expect to adjust:

- **Language and units:** English throughout, metric quantities, UK date formats.
- **Claims:** remove disease names and medicinal promises that Indian rules permit but UK rules do not.
- **Required details:** the UK contact or Responsible Person, batch number, durability date where needed, warnings and usage directions.
- **Ingredient lists:** in the format the relevant UK route requires, with allergens highlighted for food.
- **Prices:** never print Indian MRP on packs destined for Britain; it confuses shoppers and retailers.

Many brands produce a separate UK pack run rather than over-stickering, which looks more finished and avoids labels peeling in transit.

## Get stock into the UK

Moving goods in is a project of its own. In general terms you will need an EORI number for the business importing into Britain, the correct commodity codes, a customs agent or freight forwarder, and a plan for import duty and import VAT. Food supplements may face extra checks at the border. Agree who is the importer of record before the first shipment leaves. Keep commercial invoices, packing lists and testing documents together for every consignment, because missing paperwork is the commonest reason goods sit at the border.

Shipping individual parcels from India straight to UK customers looks simpler but usually disappoints. Delivery takes longer, customs can hold parcels, and buyers may receive unexpected charges. Most brands do better sending bulk stock to a UK fulfilment partner or into Amazon UK, then offering tracked delivery through Royal Mail, Evri or DPD within a few days.

## Choose a selling model

| Model | How it works | Suits | Trade-offs |
|---|---|---|---|
| UK distributor | A UK company buys stock and sells it on | Brands wanting low effort and wholesale reach | Lower margin, less control over the brand |
| Own UK store with UK stock | You run a GBP store and a UK fulfilment partner ships | Brands building direct customer relationships | You handle marketing, service and returns |
| Amazon UK | Stock sent into Amazon's network | Staples with search demand | Category approvals, document checks, competition |
| TikTok Shop UK and Etsy | Creator-led or handmade-style selling | Hair oils, soaps, ritual kits | Category limits, especially for ingestibles |
| Wholesale to shops | South Asian grocers, health food shops, salons | Familiar products with strong local demand | Slower payment terms, trade pricing |

Many entrants combine two: a UK distributor or Amazon UK for reach, and their own store for herb guides, bundles and subscriptions. Premium department stores such as Selfridges set a high bar for packaging and storytelling, which is worth studying even if you never pitch to them.

## Market to diaspora buyers first, then newcomers

Diaspora campaigns can be direct. Use the names people already search for, show where the product is made, offer bundles of everyday staples and plan around Diwali, Eid and the wedding season. Search ads on product names and Amazon UK listings often do most of the work.

Wellness newcomers need education. Build guides that explain one herb at a time, offer trial sizes and use short videos of the ritual in a British home. Every word in ads and on your site falls under the CAP Code, so describe tradition and daily use rather than health outcomes. UK online shoppers generally have a cancellation period, though opened hygiene-sealed products can be excluded. Set out returns terms in plain English and make the UK contact details easy to find, because trust is the main hurdle for an unfamiliar overseas brand.

## Building trust as a new brand in Britain

An overseas brand starts with less trust than a local one, so show your workings. Publish an About page that explains who makes the products, where and how. Put a UK contact address and customer service email on every page. Show batch testing in plain language, without turning it into a health claim. Collect reviews from early UK customers as soon as you can, and answer questions on Amazon UK and social quickly.

Content helps too. Short guides on how to use each product, how long a jar lasts and how to store oils in a cold British bathroom answer the questions newcomers have and show that you understand the market you are entering. Search engines and AI assistants pick up clear explainers, so these pages can bring steady visitors long after launch.

## Common mistakes when entering the UK

- Copying the Indian website and product descriptions, claims included
- Shipping parcels one by one from India and leaving customers to pay surprise charges
- Launching the full range instead of a handful of products with clear routes
- Treating the Indian licence as proof that the product is legal in Britain
- Skipping batch testing until a marketplace or retailer asks for results
- Running one generic campaign for diaspora buyers and wellness newcomers
- Pricing without counting duty, VAT, fulfilment and returns

## A cautious first-year plan

1. **Months one to two:** shortlist products with clear routes and known demand, appoint advisers and a Responsible Person or importer, and commission testing.
2. **Months three to four:** finalise UK labels, book freight, set up UK fulfilment and build a GBP store written for British shoppers.
3. **Months five to six:** launch to diaspora buyers through the store, search ads and Amazon UK, timed if possible before Diwali.
4. **Months seven to twelve:** add herb guides, trial sizes and creator content for wellness shoppers, review which products reorder well and decide whether a THR application is worth pursuing for a hero product.
