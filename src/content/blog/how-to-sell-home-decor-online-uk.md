If you are researching how to sell home decor online UK buyers will order with confidence, start with the obvious problem: they cannot touch the fabric, sit on the chair or hold the lamp up against their wall. British homes add another twist. Period terraces with tight staircases, flats several floors up and small modern living rooms mean a buyer worries about getting the piece inside as much as how it looks. This guide covers product choice, photography, safety rules, delivery, returns, channels and the seasonal calendar.

One caveat: we are growth marketers rather than legal advisers. The UK rules mentioned here are a starting point, so check the detail for your own products with a qualified adviser or trading standards.

## Pick products that work online

Some decor sells easily online and some fights you at every step. Before you build a large range, score each product on:

- **How well it photographs.** Texture and colour must survive a phone screen.
- **How it ships.** Compare parcel size, weight and fragility against your courier options.
- **Return risk.** Colour-sensitive textiles and statement pieces come back more often.
- **Margin after delivery.** A low-price item with costly delivery can lose money on every order.
- **Repeat or add-on potential.** Cushions, candles and throws make good basket builders for larger pieces.

Many UK brands start with smaller decor and textiles, prove demand, then add furniture once their delivery and returns processes are reliable.

## Photograph products in rooms that look like real UK homes

Photography does most of the selling. Shots on white show the product clearly, but they do not help someone judge whether a sideboard suits a terraced-house alcove.

### The photo set to aim for

- A clean cut-out on a plain background, true to colour, from several angles.
- A room shot in a space of realistic UK size, with natural light.
- A scale shot beside a person, a doorframe or a familiar object.
- Close-ups of grain, weave, stitching and joints.
- A short video turning the piece, opening drawers or showing a lamp lit at dusk.

Use the same room sets on product pages, in ads and on Pinterest so buyers recognise your products wherever they meet them.

## Give exact sizes, materials and access details

Vague descriptions cost sales and create returns. A good UK decor page answers these questions in text, not only in images:

| Detail | What to include |
|---|---|
| Dimensions | Height, width, depth, seat height, and weight |
| Packaged size | Box dimensions and the narrowest gap the item can pass through |
| Materials | Solid wood, veneer, metal, fabric composition and finish |
| Colour notes | How the colour looks in daylight versus warm bulbs |
| Care | Cleaning, sun exposure, humidity and fabric care |
| Assembly | Whether needed, tools, time and number of people |
| Safety | Fire safety labelling for upholstery, UKCA or CE marking for lighting |

Posting out fabric samples for sofas, curtains and blinds is worth the effort. Samples cut colour-related returns and give you a reason to follow up with the buyer.

## Check the product safety rules for your range

UK product safety is a broad area, and the rules depend on what you sell. In general terms:

- **Upholstered furniture and some furnishings** are covered by furniture fire safety regulations, which involve labels on the product. Buyers and marketplaces often look for them.
- **Lamps, light fittings and other electrical items** generally need to meet electrical safety rules and carry UKCA marking, with CE marking accepted in Great Britain for many product types.
- **Candles and diffusers** may need hazard labelling depending on their ingredients.
- **Everything else** falls under general product safety law, which expects products to be safe in normal use and to come with suitable warnings and instructions.

If you import, you may take on the duties of the importer, so ask suppliers for test reports and keep them on file. Product safety rules have been under review, so check current guidance before launching a new category.

## Plan packaging, delivery and breakage

### Fragile items

Glass, ceramics and mirrors need double boxing, fitted inserts and drop testing before launch. Mark parcels clearly and choose a fragile or tracked service for high-value pieces.

### Bulky items

Sofas, beds and wardrobes usually need a two-person delivery. Tell buyers whether the service goes to the room of choice, whether packaging is removed, whether assembly is included and how delivery slots are booked. Check surcharges for the Scottish Highlands and islands, Northern Ireland and other remote postcodes before you promise nationwide delivery.

### Track damage

Ask customers to report damage with photos within a set period, record breakage by product and courier, and fix the packaging or courier choice for repeat offenders.

## Write a clear returns and cancellation policy

UK distance selling rules generally give online buyers a cooling-off period to cancel and return goods, and they expect you to explain who pays for the return. Made-to-measure curtains, sofas built in a chosen fabric and personalised prints can often be excluded, but only where the item is genuinely made to the customer's own specification and you say so before the order. Separately, consumer rights law requires goods to be as described, of satisfactory quality and fit for purpose.

A clear policy states:

- How long buyers have to cancel and how to start a return.
- Whether you collect bulky items and what that costs.
- Which items are made to order and how cancellation works for them.
- How damaged deliveries are handled.

## Build trust with reviews and customer photos

A photo of your armchair in a real customer's sitting room often persuades more than a styled shoot, because it shows how the colour reads in ordinary light. Ask for a review once the item has been in use for a couple of weeks, not on the day of delivery, and make photo uploads easy on mobile.

- Send review requests after delivery and assembly, with a direct link to the product.
- Invite photos with a simple prompt such as "show us where it lives".
- Reply to critical reviews calmly and explain what you changed.
- Never write, buy or selectively hide reviews. UK consumer law treats fake and misleading reviews seriously, and marketplaces remove them.

Customer photos also make strong ad creative, with permission, and they give Pinterest a steady supply of fresh, authentic images.

## Price for delivery and basket size

Delivery can wipe out the margin on decor. Work out your true delivery cost per product type, then choose whether to absorb it, show a flat charge or charge by zone. For smaller decor, a free delivery threshold set just above your typical basket encourages add-ons. Prices must be shown including VAT for consumer sales. Where you offer instalments through a provider like Klarna or Clearpay, mention it beside the price on bigger pieces.

## Choose your UK sales channels

### Your own store

Your Shopify or WooCommerce store is where you control delivery promises, swatches, room collections and customer data. It suits high-value furniture and complete room ranges.

### Etsy and Not On The High Street

Both suit handmade, small-batch and personalised decor, prints and gifts. Buyers there expect craft stories, maker photos and clear lead times.

### Amazon UK, eBay and OnBuy

These work for smaller accessories, practical items such as storage and lighting, and replacement parts. Bulky furniture is harder to sell well on them because delivery and returns are less in your control.

### Selling beyond Great Britain

Shipping to Northern Ireland, the Republic of Ireland or the EU adds customs paperwork, different product marking expectations and higher delivery costs for bulky items. Shopify Markets can handle currencies and duties for smaller decor, but test demand with a limited range before offering furniture abroad, and make sure your returns promise still works across the border.

Big retailers such as John Lewis and IKEA set buyer expectations on delivery, returns and room inspiration. You do not need to match their scale, but your pages should answer the same questions just as clearly.

## Use Pinterest, Instagram and search

Interior buyers plan visually. Pinterest suits room boards and evergreen pins, Instagram suits room tours and maker content, and Google catches buyers searching "oak sideboard 160cm" or "boucle armchair small space". Room and style collection pages with useful buying advice rank well and can be cited by AI assistants. ## Plan around the UK home calendar

- **January.** Sales season and fresh-start projects; strong for storage and organisation.
- **Spring.** Home refreshes, Easter bank holiday garden furniture and the start of the moving season.
- **Summer.** Outdoor living and new homeowners furnishing whole rooms.
- **Autumn.** Lighting, throws and cosy textiles as evenings draw in.
- **Black Friday to Boxing Day.** Hosting, gifting and big-ticket purchases. Put your Christmas cut-off dates on the site by early November.

## Measure what matters after delivery

Revenue alone hides the real picture for decor. Review these figures by category every month:

- Margin per order after delivery, packaging, payment fees and VAT.
- Return and cancellation rate, with the reason recorded.
- Damage rate by product and by courier.
- Swatch requests that turn into orders.
- Time between first visit and purchase for larger items.

If a category looks strong on sales but weak after returns and damage, fix the page, the packaging or the delivery charge before spending more to promote it.
