Knowing how to export Ayurvedic products is mostly about one question: what does the destination country think your product is? In India, a jar of chyawanprash or a bottle of herbal oil may be licensed as an Ayurvedic medicine. In the UK, the US, the UAE or Australia, the same product could be treated as a herbal medicine, a food supplement, a cosmetic or a food, and each route has different rules for registration, labels, claims and testing. This guide explains the Indian side, the general picture in four markets, and a cautious way to start.

A note before we begin: we are ecommerce marketers, not regulatory consultants or lawyers. Rules in this area change often and depend on the exact product. Use this guide to prepare good questions, then confirm every decision with a qualified adviser in India and in the destination country.

## Why demand for Ayurveda abroad is worth the effort

Indian families in London, Leicester, New Jersey, Dubai, Sydney and Melbourne look for the brands and formulations they grew up with. Alongside them, a wider audience buys herbal teas, oils and skincare because they like plant-based routines. Both groups are reachable online, and both value reliable delivery and honest information over a small saving.

The catch is that a product that sells easily in India can be impossible to sell abroad without months of registration work. Choosing the right products and the right first country matters more than any ad campaign.

## How to export Ayurvedic products: the paperwork in India

Before any country-specific work, get the Indian side in order.

| Requirement | What it is for |
|---|---|
| Importer Exporter Code (IEC) | Needed for commercial exports from India |
| GST registration and Letter of Undertaking | Export invoicing, usually without paying IGST upfront |
| Manufacturing or loan licence | Proves the product is lawfully made in India |
| Certificate of analysis for each batch | Shows test results; buyers and regulators ask for it |
| Free sale certificate | Some countries ask for proof the product is sold in India |
| GMP evidence or a certificate of pharmaceutical product | Asked for by some regulators and importers for medicine products |
| Commercial invoice, packing list, shipping bill | Standard export and customs documents |

There is also an export promotion council for the AYUSH sector, which can help with information on markets and schemes. Ask your chartered accountant about export incentives and foreign exchange paperwork, and ask your adviser which certificates your target country will actually require.

## Classify each product for the destination country

The same product can fall into different categories in different countries. Broadly, the options are:

- **Herbal or traditional medicine.** The product is presented as treating or preventing a condition. This route usually needs registration, quality data and local responsibility, and takes the longest.
- **Food supplement or dietary supplement.** Capsules, tablets and powders taken for general wellbeing, with no medicinal claims. Rules cover ingredients, labelling and manufacturing practice.
- **Cosmetic.** Oils, soaps, ubtans and creams applied to the skin or hair for cleansing or beauty. Rules cover ingredients, safety assessment, labelling and a responsible person.
- **Food.** Teas, spice blends and some powders. Food labelling and import rules apply.

How you present the product, including the claims on the pack, website and ads, often decides the category. A herbal oil described as a massage oil may be a cosmetic; the same oil described as relieving joint pain may be a medicine. This is why claims discipline matters even more abroad than in India.

## Test every batch, especially for heavy metals

Heavy metals are the biggest concern regulators abroad raise about Ayurvedic products. Some classical formulations deliberately include mineral or metal preparations, known as bhasmas, and plant ingredients can also pick up lead, arsenic, mercury or cadmium from soil and processing. Regulators in several countries have issued warnings or import alerts about specific Ayurvedic products for this reason.

Practical steps:

- Test each batch at an accredited laboratory for heavy metals, microbial contamination and pesticide residues.
- Keep certificates of analysis on file and share them with importers.
- Avoid exporting products that contain metal or mineral preparations unless a specialist confirms they are allowed in that country.
- Check each ingredient against the destination country's lists of permitted and restricted substances.
- Review sourcing, because contamination often starts with the raw herb.

## Country by country: the general picture

The summaries below are general and simplified. Treat them as starting points for a conversation with a local adviser.

### United Kingdom

Herbal medicines in the UK are regulated by the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA). An Ayurvedic product presented as a medicine generally needs either a product licence or a traditional herbal registration (THR). The THR scheme requires evidence that the product has been used traditionally for a long period, plus quality and safety data, and allows only limited indications for minor conditions. Few Ayurvedic brands go down this route at first.

Many brands instead sell eligible products as food supplements or cosmetics. Food supplements follow food law, including rules on health claims, and cosmetics need a UK responsible person, a safety assessment and notification before sale. Advertising must also follow the UK advertising codes, which are strict on health claims. Products containing heavy metals face extra restrictions. ### United States

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has no separate category for Ayurvedic medicine. Products are usually sold as:

- **Dietary supplements**, which do not need pre-approval but must follow manufacturing practice rules, carry a Supplement Facts panel and limit themselves to structure or function statements with the required disclaimer. New ingredients may need a notification.
- **Cosmetics**, which now require facility registration and product listing under updated US cosmetics law, along with labelling and safety rules.
- **Foods**, such as teas, which need food facility registration and prior notice for imports.

A product that claims to treat or prevent a disease is treated as an unapproved drug. The FDA has published concerns about lead, mercury and arsenic in some Ayurvedic products, and some states, such as California, have their own warning requirements. A US importer of record and, for foods and supplements, a foreign supplier verification programme are usually needed.

### United Arab Emirates

In the UAE, products are classified as medicines, health or herbal products, cosmetics or foods, and registration rules depend on the category and sometimes the emirate. The federal health regulator handles medicines and many herbal or complementary products, while authorities such as Dubai Municipality approve cosmetics and some health products for the Dubai market. Labels generally need Arabic as well as English.

Most brands work through a local importer or distributor that holds the registrations. Amazon.ae and Noon list many herbal products, but sellers still need products that meet local rules.

### Australia

The Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) regulates products presented as medicines. Many herbal products are entered on the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods as listed complementary medicines (shown as AUST L on the pack). Listing requires an Australian sponsor, ingredients from a permitted list, indications from a permitted list and evidence to support them. Products with heavy metals or unusual ingredients are difficult to bring in.

Cosmetics fall under consumer and chemical import rules, and foods under the joint food standards code with New Zealand. Personal import rules for individuals do not cover a brand selling commercially.

## Adapt labels, claims and content

Once you know each product's category in a country, adapt everything around it:

- Rewrite labels to local requirements, units and languages.
- Remove claims that would push the product into the medicine category, unless it is registered as one.
- Build country pages with local currency, delivery times and duties shown clearly.
- Write product pages around ingredients, sourcing and use, with the same claim discipline as the label.
- Run ads that follow both platform health policies and local advertising codes.

## Choose a shipping and selling model

| Model | How it works | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Importer or distributor | A local company buys stock, handles registration and sells to retailers | Medicine and regulated products, larger volumes |
| Marketplace with local fulfilment | You ship stock to Amazon or Noon warehouses abroad | Products with clear categories and steady demand |
| Direct to consumer from India | Parcels go from India to each buyer | Light, low-risk products, testing demand |
| Local warehouse or 3PL | Stock sits in the country, orders ship locally | Brands with proven demand in one market |

Direct shipping from India is the quickest way to test demand, but parcels can be held at customs, and some countries do not allow personal imports of certain herbal products. Oils and liquids also face courier restrictions. Many brands start with direct shipping of teas and cosmetics, then move to a local partner. ## Common mistakes exporters make

- **Copying Indian product pages abroad.** Wording that is acceptable in India may push a product into the medicine category elsewhere.
- **Shipping before checking classification.** Parcels held at customs or destroyed cost more than an adviser's time.
- **Skipping batch testing.** One bad result can end a relationship with an importer or a marketplace.
- **Launching in many countries at once.** Each market needs its own labels, pages and support, so spread effort only once the first one works.
- **Ignoring returns and damage.** Liquids leak and glass breaks in long transit, so plan packaging and a replacement policy before the first order.

## A cautious launch plan

1. **Shortlist three to five products** that are plant-only and likely to be treated as foods or cosmetics abroad.
2. **Pick one country**, usually where you already see diaspora demand in your Indian store data or search interest.
3. **Confirm classification and requirements** with an adviser in that country.
4. **Test and label** each product to local rules, and get certificates of analysis for each batch.
5. **Launch small**, with a country page, local currency and a few ads aimed at diaspora buyers.
6. **Learn and expand** to more products or a second country once returns, customs and reviews look healthy.
