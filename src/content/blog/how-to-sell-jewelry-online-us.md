## Choose your segment

Each segment sells differently, so decide where you sit before building pages or ads.

| Segment | Typical materials | How it sells online |
|---|---|---|
| Fine jewelry | Solid gold, platinum, mined or lab-grown diamonds | Considered purchases, research over weeks, high trust needs |
| Demi-fine | Vermeil, gold filled, sterling silver, small stones | Everyday luxury and self-gifting, strong on social |

## Word metals and stones to FTC standards

The FTC's Guides for the Jewelry, Precious Metals, and Pewter Industries, usually called the Jewelry Guides, explain which jewelry descriptions the agency considers fair. They are not a statute, yet the FTC leans on them in deception cases, which makes them the working standard for US sellers. The points below are a summary, not legal advice.

### Gold

- Describe solid gold by karat, such as 10K, 14K or 18K.
- Avoid calling a piece below 10K "gold".
- A thin gold layer over another metal is gold plated or gold electroplate; a thicker bonded layer is gold filled. Each term has a meaning in the Guides, so use it only when the piece meets it.
- Vermeil means sterling silver coated with gold of a minimum karat and thickness. Plated brass is not vermeil.

### Silver and platinum

"Sterling silver" means 925 parts per thousand pure silver. Platinum has its own marking conventions for different purities. If you stamp quality marks on pieces, the federal stamping law also has rules on accuracy and on adding a registered trademark alongside the mark, so ask your adviser.

### Gemstones and pearls

- Disclose treatments such as heating, filling, coating or dyeing when they affect value or care, or need special care.
- Describe imitation stones, such as cubic zirconia sold as a diamond look-alike, as imitation or simulated.
- Describe cultured pearls as cultured.

### Lab-grown diamonds and gemstones

Lab-grown diamonds are real diamonds chemically, and many buyers choose them knowingly. The FTC wants the origin made plain, using terms like laboratory-grown or laboratory-created (or the producer's name plus 'created'), positioned next to the word diamond. Do not call them natural, and be careful with real and genuine where they could imply mined origin.

### Every place counts

Titles, Google Shopping feeds, ad headlines, marketplace listings, packaging and creator captions are all advertising. A feed title saying "gold hoops" for a plated pair is misleading even if the product page is accurate.

## Children's jewelry needs extra care

Jewelry designed mainly for children 12 and under is treated as a children's product. That brings federal lead content and surface coating limits, third-party testing at a lab accepted by the Consumer Product Safety Commission, tracking labels and a Children's Product Certificate. Some states, including California, also restrict cadmium in children's jewelry. If your range includes kids' pieces, settle testing before you advertise to parents.

Claims like "nickel-free" or "hypoallergenic" also need support. Use them only when testing or materials data backs them up.

## Build product pages that answer the questions

A jewelry shopper asks many questions before ordering. Answer all of them on the page:

- Metal and purity, written exactly as the piece is made
- Stone type, origin, treatment and, for diamonds, cut, color, clarity and carat
- Dimensions in inches and millimeters, chain length and clasp type
- Weight for pieces where it matters
- Care instructions and what to expect over time, such as plating wear
- Grading report details for significant stones
- Delivery time, engraving or made-to-order lead time
- Returns, resizing and warranty in plain words

## Photography, video and sizing

Most jewelry returns come from surprises: smaller than expected, warmer or paler gold, a stone that looked brighter on screen.

### Shoot for every hero piece

- White-background shots for marketplaces and feeds
- The piece being worn, so size is obvious at a glance
- Natural daylight as well as studio light
- Close-ups of clasps, backs, settings and stamps
- A short video showing movement and sparkle

### Sizing

Offer a printable ring sizer or a sizer mailed with a code, give a size chart with US ring sizes, and say clearly how resizing works and what it costs in time. For chains, show lengths on a model or diagram.

## Explain value, not only price

Jewelry buyers compare. A shopper looking at two similar 14K pendants wants to know why one costs more, and silence sends them to the brand that explains. Without publishing a cost breakdown, you can show what drives value: metal weight and purity, stone quality and origin, hand finishing, setting work and warranty. Gold prices move, so if your prices follow the metal market, say how often you review them. For lab-grown pieces, explain how they compare with mined stones of similar grade, and be honest that resale expectations differ.

## Payments, fraud and insured shipping

High-value jewelry attracts fraud. Use the fraud screening built into your payment provider, review orders with mismatched billing and shipping addresses, and hold unusual first orders for a quick check. Many US shoppers expect buy-now-pay-later options on larger pieces, so show financing information on product pages if you offer it.

For delivery, use insured shipping with signature on delivery for higher-value orders, discreet outer packaging with no brand name on the box, and tamper-evident seals. Photograph each high-value piece before it ships, which helps with any later dispute.

## Returns, exchanges and resizing

Write a clear returns window, the condition pieces must be in, and the exceptions: engraved, personalized and made-to-order pieces are usually final sale or exchange only. State whether resizing is free the first time, and explain any warranty on plating or settings. Clear terms reduce chargebacks and make hesitant buyers more willing to order.

## Selling into the US from another country

Many jewelry brands make pieces in India, Thailand or elsewhere and sell to US buyers. If that is you, plan for customs duties and current tariff rates on your product type, country-of-origin marking on pieces or packaging, and longer delivery times that must be stated before checkout. Low-value parcel rules have changed recently, so ask a customs broker for the current position. A US returns address, through a returns service or a small US stock holding, makes buyers far more comfortable ordering. As volume grows, many brands move stock to a US warehouse so delivery and returns feel domestic.

## The US jewelry calendar

- **Black Friday to Christmas.** The biggest gifting peak and a common time for proposals.
- **Valentine's Day.** Couples' gifts, hearts and initials.
- **Mother's Day in May.** Birthstones, name necklaces and family pieces.
- **Graduation and wedding season.** Spring and summer gifts, bands and bridal party jewelry.
- **Prime Day in July.** Relevant for Amazon sellers.
- **Diwali and the wedding season.** For South Asian American buyers, gold and bridal sets in the fall and winter.

Set engraving and shipping cut-off dates early and publish them on product pages.

## Getting your first buyers

Start with your own audience and people who already know your work. Collect photo reviews from early buyers, and ask creators to style pieces honestly and to disclose gifts, payments and affiliate links plainly, as the FTC requires. Never buy or fake reviews. Search ads on precise phrases, like "oval solitaire in 14K white gold", usually convert better than broad terms.

- Segment chosen and product range sized to it

- Fraud screening, insured shipping and signature rules in place
- Return, resizing and warranty terms written plainly
- Feed attributes accurate for Google Shopping
