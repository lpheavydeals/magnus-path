Online brands ask us how to run a pop up shop in London more often than almost any other offline question. A pop-up lets people touch, smell and try what they have only seen on a screen. It gives you honest feedback in a weekend, content for your social channels and, done well, a list of new customers who keep buying online long after the stall is packed away. Done badly, it is an expensive weekend of standing around.

This guide covers choosing a space, planning costs, licences and insurance, taking payments and, most importantly, turning pop-up buyers into online customers. Licensing and insurance points are general; check them with the venue, your council and your insurer.

## Start with one clear goal

Decide what the pop-up is for before you look at spaces. Typical goals are:

- **Testing:** seeing how people react to a new product, scent, colour or price
- **Selling:** clearing seasonal stock or selling gifts before Christmas
- **Meeting customers:** turning online followers into people who have met the brand
- **Building a list:** collecting emails and phone numbers for future online sales
- **Making content:** filming real reactions and customer questions for ads and social

One main goal shapes everything else: the space you choose, the stock you bring and how you measure success.

## Where to run a pop-up in London

London has spaces for every stage of brand. Treat the names below as examples of types of space, not recommendations.

### Markets

Weekly and weekend markets are where most brands start. Old Spitalfields Market in east London has regular trader days for makers and independent brands. Neighbourhood markets, makers' fairs and seasonal Christmas markets across the boroughs bring in local buyers who enjoy finding something new. Pitches are short, set-up is simple and you learn quickly, but space is tight and outdoor stalls are at the mercy of the weather.

### Container and food-hall sites

Sites such as Boxpark, with locations in Shoreditch, Croydon and Wembley, mix food, drink and retail units. They draw a younger crowd and suit brands with strong visual identities, such as streetwear, accessories and beauty.

### Short-let shops and centres

Shopping districts such as Seven Dials in Covent Garden and large centres such as Westfield London and Westfield Stratford City offer short-term retail units from time to time. They bring huge footfall and a proper shop front, but terms are stricter, fit-out expectations are higher and costs reflect the location. They suit brands that already have proven products and a clear reason for people to visit.

### Shared and hosted spaces

Some independent shops, cafés, salons and studios host brands for a few days or share a unit between several brands. These can be a gentle first step, especially when the host's customers match yours.

## Plan costs without guesswork

Pop-up costs vary hugely across London, so we will not quote figures. Instead, list every cost driver and ask each venue for full written terms:

| Cost driver | What to ask |
|---|---|
| Rent or pitch fee | Is it per day, week or a share of sales? |
| Deposit and service charges | What is refundable, and when? |
| Fit-out and display | What furniture, lighting and power are included? |
| Stock | How much will you bring, and where will spare stock wait? |
| Staff | Who covers breaks, opening and packing down? |
| Insurance | What levels of cover does the venue require? |
| Payments | What do card fees and readers cost on your plan? |
| Transport | Van hire, parking, congestion and loading times |
| Promotion | Printed cards, social ads and creator invitations |

Set a simple target before you sign: the sales, sign-ups or conversations that would make the pop-up worthwhile. If you plan an event offer, check the margin first with our [discount calculator](/tools/discount-calculator/).

## Licences and insurance in general terms

Managed markets, shopping centres and hosted shops usually hold the premises licences and ask you to sign trader terms. Trading on a public street or a council-run market normally needs a street trading licence from the borough. Selling food, serving drinks, playing music or running events can each need further permissions or a food business registration, so ask the venue and council early.

Most venues ask for public liability insurance. Product liability cover matters if your products go on skin, in mouths or near children, and employer's liability insurance is generally required if you employ staff. Check that your policy covers stock on site and in transit.

## Stock, staff and display

A pop-up feels busy or empty depending on how it is set up, not only on footfall.

### Stock

Bring your hero products in depth and a small selection of the rest. Running out of the item everyone asks about is a missed sale, while bringing the whole catalogue clutters the table. Keep a simple sheet of what went out and what came back, and agree where spare stock waits, whether in a van, a storeroom or a nearby unit.

### Staff

Plan at least two people for busy days, so one can talk to visitors while the other takes payments and restocks. Brief everyone on the product story, the event offer, the email sign-up and the answers to common questions. Rotate breaks so the stall is never left with a single tired person.

### Display

Make the products easy to touch and try. Use height so items are visible from a distance, put prices clearly on everything and keep one tester or sample per product. A simple sign with your brand name and one line about what you do helps people decide whether to stop.

## Take payments and track every sale

Use a card reader linked to your online store's point of sale, so stock levels, sales and customer details sit in one system. Shoppers expect contactless and phone wallets, and many carry no cash. Before opening:

- [ ] Test the reader on site, including mobile data or Wi-Fi in the exact spot
- [ ] Charge a spare reader and a power bank
- [ ] Keep payment links ready in case the signal drops
- [ ] Load all pop-up products into the point of sale with correct prices
- [ ] Set an event discount code to track online orders that follow

## How to run a pop up shop in London that grows online sales

The weekend's takings are only part of the return. The real value is the people who come back to your store afterwards.

### Capture emails at the till

Ask every buyer for an email with a clear reason: a digital receipt, a care or usage guide, or a thank-you offer on their next online order. Ask browsers too. A tablet sign-up form or a QR code on the counter makes it quick. Tell people what they will receive and how often, and keep consent records in line with UK GDPR.

### Build a landing page for the event

Print a card with a QR code that leads to a page made for the pop-up: the products on display, the event offer, reviews and simple delivery information. Tag the link so you can see in analytics how many visitors and orders came from the event.

### Follow up within a day

Send a welcome email within 24 hours while the visit is fresh. Follow with a short guide on using the product, then an invitation to reorder or try something related. Run social ads to people who visited the event page, using photos and videos taken at the stall. ### Record what people asked

Keep a notebook or shared note of every question, objection and comment. These become product page improvements, FAQ answers and ad hooks. Skincare and beauty brands in particular learn which textures and scents sell when people can try them, which is why pop-ups feature in many plans our [London skincare marketing team](/london/skincare-marketing-agency/) works on.

## Measure what worked

After the event, compare results against the goal you set:

1. Sales on the day, by product
2. Emails and phone numbers collected
3. Online orders using the event code in the following weeks
4. Social content produced and how it performed
5. Questions and feedback that should change your store or products

Then decide whether to repeat the same space, try a different type of venue or wait until a seasonal moment such as Christmas.

## Timing your pop-up

London's calendar shapes footfall. The weeks before Christmas bring gift buyers to markets and centres, but pitches book up early and competition for attention is high. Spring and early summer weekends suit outdoor markets, while January is quiet but cheaper and calmer for testing. Match the date to your products: gifts before Christmas, Valentine's Day and Mother's Day, summer products from May, and launches when you have time to follow up properly afterwards. Avoid clashing with a big event near the venue unless its crowd suits your brand.

## Common pop-up mistakes

- Choosing a famous location that does not match your buyer
- Bringing too much stock and too few staff
- No plan for collecting emails, so visitors are lost after the weekend
- Untested card readers in a hall with weak signal
- No event code, so online sales from the pop-up go unmeasured
- Waiting a week to follow up
