Working out how to sell Ayurvedic products in Australia is a different task from selling them in India. This guide is for two readers: Indian manufacturers who want Australian customers, and Australian importers or distributors who want to bring Ayurvedic brands in. Australia has a large and growing South Asian community and a mature wellness market, but it also has strict therapeutic goods law and some of the tightest biosecurity controls in the world. Brands that plan for both can build a steady business. Brands that ship first and ask later often lose stock at the border or have listings removed.

Everything below is general information. We are an ecommerce growth team based in India, not regulatory or customs advisers, so confirm each step with a TGA consultant, a customs broker and your sponsor.

## Who buys Ayurveda in Australia

Two groups make up most of the demand.

- **South Asian communities.** Indian, Sri Lankan, Nepali and Fijian Indian families in Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, Perth and Adelaide already know chyawanprash, herbal hair oils, kumkumadi and triphala. They want familiar products, reliable stock and fair delivery times.
- **Wellness shoppers.** Australians who buy turmeric lattes, yoga classes and adaptogen blends are curious about Ayurveda but need it explained. They respond to ingredient stories, rituals and clear quality information.

A third, smaller group is practitioners: Ayurvedic consultants, naturopaths and massage therapists who buy oils and powders in larger packs. They can become steady wholesale customers once you are established.

## How to sell Ayurvedic products in Australia: give every product a route

Australia does not have a single "Ayurveda" category. Each product enters under a route set by its ingredients, its form and the claims made for it.

### The cosmetic route

Hair oils, body oils, soaps, ubtan and face packs are often cosmetics, provided they make no therapeutic claims. They need ingredient labelling under the mandatory cosmetics standard, and the Australian business that imports them may need to register with AICIS, the scheme that covers chemical ingredients introduced into Australia, including those in cosmetics. This is usually the simplest route to start with.

### The food route

Herbal teas, spice blends, ghee and some pastes may be foods. Imported food can be inspected under the Imported Food Inspection Scheme, must meet the Food Standards Code and needs Australian labelling, including country of origin. Health claims on food are limited, so a tea sold as food should be described by taste, origin and tradition.

### The listed medicine route

Tablets, capsules, churnas and classical formulations sold for a health purpose are usually therapeutic goods. Most lower-risk herbal medicines are listed on the ARTG by an Australian sponsor, carry an AUST L number and may only use indications from the TGA's permitted list. Overseas manufacturers of medicines generally need to show that they meet acceptable manufacturing standards, which the sponsor arranges with the TGA. Budget time for this; it is rarely quick.

### Products to hold back

Mineral and metal-based preparations such as bhasmas, formulas containing scheduled herbs, and anything with claims about serious illness are wiser to leave out of a first Australian range. Some may never be suitable.

## Biosecurity and import checks

Australia checks plant and animal material closely. The Department of Agriculture, Fisheries and Forestry (DAFF) publishes import conditions in BICON, its online database. Before you ship, look up every ingredient, not just the product name.

- **Plant material and seeds:** dried herbs, roots and whole seeds may need permits, treatment or inspection, and some cannot enter at all.
- **Dairy and honey:** ghee, chyawanprash and honey-based pastes can face extra conditions.
- **Documents:** commercial invoices, packing lists and ingredient declarations should match exactly; vague descriptions such as "herbal powder" invite holds.
- **First shipments:** expect inspections and allow time in your launch plan.

A licensed customs broker in Australia is worth the cost for commercial consignments. They can tell you which conditions apply and lodge the right entries.

## Test every batch and keep the paperwork

Quality is the strongest selling point an Ayurvedic brand has in Australia, and the weakest point when it goes wrong. The TGA has published warnings over the years about imported Ayurvedic medicines found to contain heavy metals, and Australian buyers have read those stories.

- Test each batch at an accredited laboratory for lead, mercury, arsenic and cadmium, along with microbes and pesticide residues
- Keep the reports by batch number, so any parcel can be traced
- Share a plain summary with your sponsor, importer and marketplaces
- Put a short "how we test" page on your site in everyday language

## Relabel for Australia

An Indian label rarely works unchanged. Expect to adjust:

- ingredient lists to the Australian cosmetic or food format
- AUST L numbers, sponsor details and required warnings for medicines
- the name and Australian address of the importer or sponsor
- country of origin wording for foods
- claims that are normal in India but read as therapeutic claims here

Make the label, the product page and every ad describe the product the same way. Mismatches are a common reason for marketplace takedowns.

## Get stock into Australia and choose a selling model

| Model | How it works | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Direct from India | Parcels sent to each customer | Cosmetics and some foods while testing demand |
| Importer and local warehouse | Bulk shipment cleared once, orders sent from Australia | Brands ready for faster delivery and Afterpay |
| Amazon AU with local stock | Stock sent to Amazon's Australian warehouses | Familiar staples with existing search demand |
| Wholesale | Indian grocers, health food stores, practitioners | Building presence alongside online sales |

Direct shipping is tempting but limited. Australians are used to quick domestic delivery, and herbal medicines advertised to the Australian public generally need to be on the ARTG regardless of where they ship from. Most brands that grow move to an importer and local warehouse within the first year. ## Market to diaspora buyers first, then wellness newcomers

Diaspora buyers already want what you make, so they are the fastest route to first sales and reviews. Reach them through Meta ads in the main cities, community creators, Indian grocers and festival events. Diwali is the biggest gifting moment, followed by wedding season, when ubtan kits and gift hampers sell well.

Wellness newcomers need education. Search content explaining one herb or ritual at a time, trial sizes and clear "how to use" videos work better than a full catalogue. Christmas, Click Frenzy, Black Friday, Mother's Day in May and EOFY in June all suit gift sets for this group. ### Channels to plan for

- **Your own Shopify store:** the place to explain herbs, sell bundles and run subscriptions
- **Amazon AU:** reorders of known products, once local stock is in place
- **eBay AU and Etsy:** gift packs, handmade soaps and small-batch oils
- **TikTok Shop:** creator demos of cosmetic products, if it is open to your business and category

## Price, GST and delivery promises

Australian shoppers expect prices to include GST, and imported goods generally attract GST at the border or at checkout, depending on how they are sold. Work out your landed cost per unit before setting a retail price: freight, broker fees, testing, relabelling, warehouse charges, marketplace fees and returns all add up. Products that look generous in India can turn thin once these are counted.

Delivery promises need the same care. If you ship from India, say so plainly on the product page, give a realistic window and explain what happens if a parcel is held for inspection. If you ship from an Australian warehouse, give regional estimates, since Perth, Darwin and country towns take longer than the east-coast cities. Afterpay and Zip are widely used, but they suit local stock better than long international waits.

## Building trust as a new brand in Australia

An unfamiliar Indian name on a jar is a hard sell to a wellness shopper, even if the brand is well known at home. Fill that gap with evidence rather than adjectives:

- An "about us" page that shows the factory, the founders and the region the herbs come from
- Batch testing summaries and the name of the Australian importer or sponsor
- Clear contact details and a returns process run from Australia
- Reviews from Australian buyers, gathered from your first diaspora customers
- Honest descriptions of taste, smell and texture, so nobody is surprised

Consumer rights under the Australian Consumer Law apply to your products once they are sold here, so faulty, mislabelled or damaged goods must be put right. Make sure your importer and your returns process can handle that from day one.

## Common mistakes when entering Australia

- Shipping medicines before a sponsor and ARTG listing are in place
- Describing products by the conditions they treat, copied from Indian packaging
- Sending dried herbs or seeds without checking BICON
- Skipping heavy metal testing to save time on a first batch
- Promising Indian delivery speeds to Australian buyers
- Running paid influencer content for listed medicines, which the advertising code restricts
- Launching the whole catalogue instead of a few products with a clear route

## A cautious first year

Pick three to six products with a simple route and proven diaspora demand. Test and relabel them, bring one consolidated shipment in through a broker, and launch on your own store and Amazon AU. Spend the first months learning which products reorder, then add trial sizes and education for wellness shoppers. Start the sponsor and listing work for medicines in parallel, so they are ready for year two rather than holding up year one.
