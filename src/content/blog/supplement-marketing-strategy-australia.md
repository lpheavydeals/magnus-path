A supplement marketing strategy Australia's regulators and ad platforms will both accept begins somewhere unglamorous: a spreadsheet listing every product, whether it is a listed medicine or a food, and exactly which claims it may carry. Get that right and creative, channels and offers become far easier to plan. Skip it and you lose months to rejected ads, removed listings and complaints. This playbook is written for Australian founders and marketing leads who want growth that lasts well past January.

We run ecommerce growth, not regulatory advice. Use the rule summaries here as a starting point and confirm the details with a qualified adviser.

## Build a claims register before you build campaigns

Make one sheet, shared by everyone who writes copy, with a row for each product:

| Column | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Product type | Listed complementary medicine (AUST L) or food, such as a formulated supplementary sports food |
| Allowed claims | Permitted indications for medicines, or food health claims for foods, with exact wording |
| Evidence held | Where the supporting file lives and who signed it off |
| Required statements | Label prompts and warnings that must travel with ads |
| Words to avoid | Disease terms, "detox", "boosts immunity", weight promises |
| Channel notes | Amazon AU category status, platform restrictions, creator limits |

The register becomes the single source for ads, listings, emails and creator briefs. When someone suggests a new angle, it goes into the register for review before it goes anywhere near a campaign.

## Pick one buyer and one reason to choose you

Australian supplement shelves are crowded, and pharmacy chains such as Chemist Warehouse set shopper expectations on price and range. A direct brand rarely wins by being cheaper. It wins by being specific.

Choose a primary buyer: the recreational lifter who wants a clean-tasting protein, the busy parent who wants one simple daily routine, the trail runner who needs electrolytes that sit well on a long run. Then choose one reason to pick you that you can state without a health claim. Format, flavour, ingredient transparency, Australian manufacture where that is true, or a neat refill system can each carry a brand.

## Write ads that pass review and still sell

### What tends to get rejected

- Anything suggesting a product treats or prevents a condition.
- Weight loss promises, body-shaming hooks and before-and-after images.
- Testimonials about health results, particularly for listed medicines, where the TGA code restricts paid and gifted endorsements.
- Ads that reach under-18s, or audiences built on sensitive health interests.
- Landing pages that make claims the ad carefully avoided. Platforms and regulators read both.

### Angles that usually work

- **Routine.** The morning capsule, the post-training shake, the sachet in the hiking pack.
- **Ingredients made plain.** What is in each serve and why that form was chosen.
- **Taste and texture.** Flavour reveals, shaker tests, gummy close-ups.
- **Proof of quality.** Batch testing, full label shots, where and how it is made.
- **Convenience.** Subscriptions, travel packs, letterbox-friendly delivery.

For listed medicines, the advertising code applies to every public-facing message, and certain statements must appear. For food-type products, the AANA Code of Ethics applies and Ad Standards will consider complaints from the public or competitors.

## Plan a channel mix where each channel has a job

### Meta and Instagram

Meta suits introducing the brand through short video and carousels, then following up with people who viewed products. Broad audiences with strong creative often outperform narrow interest stacks, which have shrunk for health topics anyway.

### Google Search and Shopping

People searching for "magnesium glycinate" or "whey isolate chocolate" are close to buying. A clean Shopping feed with GST-inclusive AUD prices, accurate titles and sharp images earns those clicks. Search ads face health policy limits, so keep copy factual.

### Amazon AU

Amazon.com.au is often where Australians compare and reorder. Listings with label images, A+ content and genuine reviews matter more than bids. Sponsored Products can defend your brand name and capture category searches once the listing converts well.

### TikTok and TikTok Shop

Short creator demos suit gummies, electrolytes and new flavours. TikTok Shop availability and its health category rules have shifted, so confirm what is open to your business in Australia before you plan around it.

### eBay AU and Kogan

Useful for multi-packs, bundles and clearance, provided pricing stays consistent with your own store.

### Email and SMS

Often the most profitable channel for a supplement brand, because reorders come from people who already trust you. Collect consent properly under the Spam Act and send messages people expect to receive.

## Use creators carefully

Creators are powerful in this category, and the rules around them are stricter in Australia than many founders realise.

1. Check the product type first. For listed medicines, people you pay or gift product to generally cannot give testimonials, so content should focus on demonstration rather than personal results.
2. Write a brief that lists permitted wording, banned words and required statements.
3. Ask for scripts or rough cuts before anything is published.
4. Make sure paid content is clearly identifiable as advertising, as the AANA codes require.
5. Keep a record of what was approved and when.

Creators who are also health professionals face extra limits on endorsing therapeutic goods, so take advice before you sign one.

## Publish content that answers real questions

Australians ask search engines and AI assistants things like "what is the difference between whey and whey isolate" or "how much vitamin D is in one capsule". Plain, careful answers on your site earn search traffic and citations in AI answers. Point to independent sources on health topics, avoid medical promises and keep product mentions factual.

Formats that work well include ingredient explainers, how-to-use guides, comparisons between your own products and clear pages about testing and manufacture.

## Make retention the centre of the plan

A first order rarely pays for itself once ad costs are counted. The second, third and fourth orders do.

### Retention checklist

- [ ] Reminder timed to when a pack is likely to run out.
- [ ] Subscribe-and-save with easy online pause, skip and cancel.
- [ ] Plain renewal terms; subscription practices are an ACCC focus.
- [ ] A larger pack offered after a second reorder.
- [ ] Flavour rotation or sample sachets to keep the routine interesting.
- [ ] Review requests after a few weeks of use.
- [ ] One win-back message for lapsed buyers, not a weekly barrage.

## Make landing pages carry the same message

An ad that passes review can still fail if the page behind it says something different. Reviewers at Meta, Google and TikTok look at the destination, and so do regulators when a complaint comes in. More practically, a buyer who clicked on a flavour video expects to land on that flavour, not a home page.

- Send each ad angle to a page that repeats its promise in the first line.
- Show the full label, serving size and serves per pack near the top.
- Put the AUST L number, or the sports food description, where it is easy to find.
- Answer the three questions your customer service inbox hears most.
- Keep the add-to-cart button, delivery estimate and Afterpay message visible on mobile.

Page speed matters too. Many supplement shoppers arrive from social apps on a phone, often on patchy mobile data, and a slow page loses them before the label loads.

## Use offers that protect the brand

Constant discounting trains buyers to wait for the next sale. Prefer bundles, larger packs, a free shaker or sample sachets, and subscription savings. If you show a "was" price, it must be one you genuinely charged for a reasonable period, or you risk misleading consumers under the Australian Consumer Law. Afterpay and similar BNPL options can help with larger stacks, as long as the total cost stays clear.

## Time campaigns to the Australian year

| Moment | Typical angle |
|---|---|
| New Year | Routines, gym returns, starter bundles |
| Autumn and winter | General wellbeing, with extra care on wording |
| EOFY (June) | Stock clearance and multi-packs |
| Father's Day (September) | Sports nutrition gift packs |
| Click Frenzy and Black Friday | Bundles and subscription sign-ups |
| Christmas and Boxing Day | Gift sets, then clearance and New Year preparation |

## Measure what actually matters

- Cost per first order by channel, not only return on ad spend.
- Repeat purchase rate at 60, 90 and 180 days.
- Contribution margin after fees, freight, GST and returns.
- Ad rejection rate, as an early sign your copy is drifting.
- Share of revenue from subscribers.

## Supplement marketing mistakes to avoid

- **Copying a big brand's wording.** It may hold a different listing, or it may simply be taking a risk you do not want.
- **Writing creative before the claims register.** You end up rewriting everything after the first round of rejections.
- **Judging creators on one post.** Test several briefs and measure orders over weeks, not likes on the day.
- **Scaling the wrong products.** A hero product that sells once and stops can hide a weaker business underneath.

## Supplement marketing strategy Australia: a 90-day plan

**Days 1 to 30.** Build the claims register, fix product pages, set up tracking, and launch email capture and reorder reminders.

**Days 31 to 60.** Launch Meta and Google with three to five creative angles, brief two or three creators, and open Amazon AU once approved.

**Days 61 to 90.** Cut weak angles, put more behind the ones that bring repeat buyers, add subscribe-and-save offers and prepare the next seasonal moment.
